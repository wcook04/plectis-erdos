\begin{abstract}
\noindent
Sylvester's sequence $2,3,7,43,1807,\ldots$ satisfies
$a_{n+1}=a_n^{2}-a_n+1$ and has reciprocal sum $1$.  Erd\H{o}s asked whether a
rapidly growing integer sequence with rational reciprocal sum must satisfy that
recurrence eventually.  We formalise in Lean the integer state system in which
the question becomes an assertion about a single centred error.

Writing $D_{n+1}=a_nD_n$, $C_{n+1}=a_nC_n-D_n$ and $E_n=D_n-(a_n-1)C_n$, we
prove the update law $C_{n+1}=C_n-E_n$ and the defect identity
\[
 \bigl(a_{n+1}-(a_n^{2}-a_n+1)\bigr)\,C_{n+1}=a_n^{2}E_n-E_{n+1},
\]
and deduce that two consecutive vanishing errors with $C_{n+1}\ne0$ force the
exact Sylvester step; in sequence form, eventual vanishing of $E$ together with
eventual nonvanishing of $C$ forces the recurrence eventually.  Separately we
prove the descent that closes the one-sided case: a nonnegative integer state
with $C_{n+1}+E_n=C_n$ has $E_n=0$ eventually.  Finally we prove that survival
through $h$ forced updates of the normalised constant-negative template depends
only on the initial state modulo $(h+1)!$.

Problem~\#243 is open and nothing here decides it.  The surviving case is
exactly the one the descent does not reach, namely a centred state that is
negative infinitely often; we neither exclude such an orbit nor construct one.
An exact search over initial states below $5000$, reduced by the scale
equivariance we also prove, produced forced prefixes of length $17$ and no
longer; that is a computation, not a theorem.  The identification of the state
system with reciprocal tails is exposition here and is not part of the
formalised statements.
\end{abstract}

\section{The problem}\label{sec:problem}

Sylvester's sequence $2,3,7,43,1807,\ldots$ is generated by
$a_{n}=a_{n-1}^{2}-a_{n-1}+1$ and has reciprocal sum $1$; the recurrence is
exactly the statement that the tail beyond $a_{n-1}$ equals $1/(a_{n-1}-1)$.
Erd\H{o}s Problem~\#243 asserts that this is the only way a sequence of that
growth can have a rational reciprocal sum:

\begin{problem}[Erd\H{o}s \#243]\label{res:problem}
Let $1\le a_1<a_2<\cdots$ be a sequence of integers with
\[
 \lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{a_n}{a_{n-1}^{2}}=1
 \qquad\text{and}\qquad
 \sum\frac{1}{a_n}\in\Q .
\]
Then $a_n=a_{n-1}^{2}-a_{n-1}+1$ for all sufficiently large $n$.
\end{problem}

\noindent See~\cite[p.~64]{erdosgraham1980} and~\cite[p.~105]{erdos1988};
numbering and status follow Bloom's catalogue~\cite{erdosproblems}.  The
problem is open.  Below we index the same recurrence as
$a_{n+1}=a_n^{2}-a_n+1$, which is the shift the formal sources use; the two
forms are the same statement.

Write $\sylnext(a)=a^{2}-a+1$ throughout, the
\lword{Erdos243/ReciprocalTailRigidity.lean}{19}{sylvesterNext}{Sylvester
successor}.

\paragraph{Relation to prior work.}
Two results bound what is new here, and both are stronger than anything below.
Erd\H{o}s and Straus proved that if $\lim a_n/a_{n-1}^{2}=1$, the reciprocal
sum is rational, and $a_n$ does not satisfy the recurrence, then
\[
 \limsup_{n\to\infty}\ \frac{[a_1,\ldots,a_n]}{a_{n+1}}
 \left(\frac{a_n^{2}}{a_{n+1}}-1\right)>0,
\]
where $[a_1,\ldots,a_n]$ is the least common
multiple~\cite{erdosstraus1964}.  Duverney proved a conditional form of the
problem itself: if $\sum_{n\ge0}\bigl(a_{n+1}/a_n^{2}-1\bigr)$ converges, then
the reciprocal sum is rational if and only if the recurrence holds for all
large $n$~\cite{duverney2001}.  Neither is formalised here, and the results
below do not recover either of them.  The statement of Problem~\#243 has also
been formalised before, as a conjecture with an unfilled proof, in the
\emph{formal-conjectures} collection; that is a statement of the question.  No
claim of priority is made for anything below.

\paragraph{Why a state system.}
The hypothesis is asymptotic and the conclusion is exact, so the argument must
convert an analytic rate into an integer obstruction.  The conversion used here
is the classical one: clear denominators along the sequence, so that
rationality makes a tail integral, and then centre that integer at the value it
would take on Sylvester's sequence.  What remains is a single integer error
whose sign controls everything.  Section~\ref{sec:state} sets this up,
Section~\ref{sec:defect} proves the rigidity step, Section~\ref{sec:descent}
closes the case where the error is nonnegative, and Section~\ref{sec:residue}
reduces the surviving case to a finite-horizon residue computation.

\section{The centred integer state}\label{sec:state}

Fix integers and define, for $a,D,C\in\Z$,
\[
 \den(a,D)=aD,
 \quad
 \tail(a,D,C)=aC-D,
 \quad
 \ctr(a,D,C)=D-(a-1)C .
\]
These are the
\lword{Erdos243/ReciprocalTailRigidity.lean}{23}{nextDenState}{denominator
update}, the
\lword{Erdos243/ReciprocalTailRigidity.lean}{27}{nextTailState}{tail update},
and the
\lword{Erdos243/ReciprocalTailRigidity.lean}{31}{centeredState}{centred state}.
Given $(a_n)$ we write $D_{n+1}=\den(a_n,D_n)$, $C_{n+1}=\tail(a_n,D_n,C_n)$
and $E_n=\ctr(a_n,D_n,C_n)$.

\begin{proposition}[update law]\label{res:update}
$\tail(a,D,C)=C-\ctr(a,D,C)$; that is, $C_{n+1}=C_n-E_n$.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
$C-\bigl(D-(a-1)C\bigr)=aC-D$.
\end{proof}

\noindent Formalised as the
\lword{Erdos243/ReciprocalTailRigidity.lean}{39}{nextTailState_eq_sub_centered}{update
law}.  The state therefore moves by exactly the error at each step, which is
what makes the sign of $E$ decisive.

\paragraph{The intended reading, which is not formalised.}
Let $T_n=\sum_{k\ge n}1/a_k$ and suppose $T_0\in\Q$.  Put $D_0$ equal to a
common denominator and $D_n=D_0a_0\cdots a_{n-1}$, and set $C_n=D_nT_n$.  Then
$C_n\in\Z$ for every $n$, and from $T_n=1/a_n+T_{n+1}$ one gets
$C_{n+1}=a_nC_n-D_n$, which is the tail update.  On Sylvester's sequence
$T_n=1/(a_n-1)$ exactly, so $D_n=(a_n-1)C_n$ and $E_n=0$: the centred state
measures deviation from the Sylvester tail identity, and it vanishes
identically on the Sylvester orbit.

None of this paragraph is formalised.  The Lean module contains no series, no
rationality hypothesis, and no growth hypothesis; it proves identities and
implications about the integer system above.  A reader should treat the
identification as motivation for the definitions and not as a checked step.

\begin{proposition}[scale equivariance]\label{res:scale}
For every $s,a,D,C\in\Z$,
\[
 \den(a,sD)=s\,\den(a,D),\quad
 \tail(a,sD,sC)=s\,\tail(a,D,C),\quad
 \ctr(a,sD,sC)=s\,\ctr(a,D,C).
\]
\end{proposition}

\noindent Formalised as the
\lword{Erdos243/ReciprocalTailRigidity.lean}{47}{nextDenState_scale}{denominator
equivariance}, the
\lword{Erdos243/ReciprocalTailRigidity.lean}{52}{nextTailState_scale}{tail
equivariance}, and the
\lword{Erdos243/ReciprocalTailRigidity.lean}{57}{centeredState_scale}{centred
equivariance}.  The system therefore depends only on the ray through
$(D,C)$, and a search over initial states may quotient by common scaling.  We
use this in Section~\ref{sec:residue}; it removed a family of duplicate
candidates that differ only by a scalar.

\section{The defect identity and the rigidity step}\label{sec:defect}

Write $\defect(a,a')=a'-\sylnext(a)$ for the deviation of the next term from
the Sylvester successor, the
\lword{Erdos243/ReciprocalTailRigidity.lean}{35}{sylvesterDefect}{Sylvester
defect}.

\begin{theorem}[defect identity]\label{res:defect}
For all $a,a',D,C\in\Z$,
\[
 \defect(a,a')\cdot\tail(a,D,C)
 =a^{2}\,\ctr(a,D,C)-\ctr\bigl(a',\den(a,D),\tail(a,D,C)\bigr),
\]
that is, $\Delta_n\,C_{n+1}=a_n^{2}E_n-E_{n+1}$.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
Both sides expand to
$a'aC-a'D-a^{3}C+a^{2}D+a^{2}C-aD-aC+D$.
\end{proof}

\noindent Formalised as the
\lword{Erdos243/ReciprocalTailRigidity.lean}{64}{sylvesterDefect_mul_nextTailState}{defect
identity}.  It is a polynomial identity in four indeterminates, with no
hypothesis of any kind.

The identity is the whole rigidity mechanism.  The left side vanishes exactly
when the sequence takes the Sylvester step or the state $C_{n+1}$ vanishes; the
right side vanishes when two consecutive errors do.  Reading it in the second
direction gives the following.

\begin{theorem}[two vanishing errors force the step]\label{res:step}
Let $a,a',D,C\in\Z$ with $\tail(a,D,C)\ne0$.  If
$\ctr(a,D,C)=0$ and $\ctr\bigl(a',\den(a,D),\tail(a,D,C)\bigr)=0$, then
$a'=\sylnext(a)$.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
By Theorem~\ref{res:defect} the product $\defect(a,a')\cdot\tail(a,D,C)$ is
zero, and the second factor is nonzero by hypothesis, so $\defect(a,a')=0$.
\end{proof}

\noindent Formalised as the
\lword{Erdos243/ReciprocalTailRigidity.lean}{76}{sylvesterNext_eq_of_centered_zero}{local
rigidity step}.  The hypothesis $C_{n+1}\ne0$ is not removable: at
$C_{n+1}=0$ the identity gives no information about $a'$, and indeed a state
that has reached zero constrains nothing further.

\begin{theorem}[sequence form]\label{res:eventual}
Let $a,D,C:\N\to\Z$ satisfy $D_{n+1}=\den(a_n,D_n)$ and
$C_{n+1}=\tail(a_n,D_n,C_n)$ for every $n$.  Suppose that
$E_n=\ctr(a_n,D_n,C_n)$ vanishes for all sufficiently large $n$, and that
$C_{n+1}\ne0$ for all sufficiently large $n$.  Then there is an $N$ with
\[
 a_{n+1}=a_n^{2}-a_n+1\qquad\text{for every }n\ge N .
\]
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
Take $N$ to be the larger of the two thresholds and apply
Theorem~\ref{res:step} at each $n\ge N$, using the transition equations to
identify $C_{n+1}$ with $\tail(a_n,D_n,C_n)$ and $E_{n+1}$ with
$\ctr\bigl(a_{n+1},\den(a_n,D_n),\tail(a_n,D_n,C_n)\bigr)$.
\end{proof}

\noindent Formalised as the
\lword{Erdos243/ReciprocalTailRigidity.lean}{94}{sylvesterNext_eventually_of_centered_zero}{eventual
Sylvester recurrence}.  Both hypotheses are eventual, and the conclusion is
eventual; no compactness or limiting argument is involved, and the two
thresholds are merged explicitly.

\section{The nonnegative case}\label{sec:descent}

Theorem~\ref{res:eventual} needs the error to vanish eventually.  When the
state and the error are nonnegative that is automatic, by descent.

\begin{theorem}[descent]\label{res:descent}
Let $C,E:\N\to\N$ satisfy $C_{n+1}+E_n=C_n$ for every $n$.  Then $E_n=0$ for
all sufficiently large $n$.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
The relation forces $C_{n+1}\le C_n$, so $C$ is a nonincreasing sequence of
natural numbers and is therefore eventually equal to its minimum value; beyond
that index $C_{n+1}=C_n$, and the relation gives $E_n=0$.
\end{proof}

\noindent Formalised as the
\lword{Erdos243/ReciprocalTailRigidity.lean}{132}{centeredState_eventually_zero}{stabilisation
of the nonnegative error}, on the
\lword{Erdos243/ReciprocalTailRigidity.lean}{114}{antitone_nat_eventually_constant}{eventual
constancy of a nonincreasing sequence}.

The two hypotheses of Theorem~\ref{res:descent} are exactly the one-sidedness:
$C$ and $E$ take values in $\N$.  The relation $C_{n+1}+E_n=C_n$ is
Proposition~\ref{res:update} rewritten, so the theorem is the descent step of
every one-sided criterion for this problem.  It is stated for abstract
$\N$-valued sequences, and instantiating it at the orbit of
Section~\ref{sec:state} requires knowing that the state and the error are
nonnegative there, which is not proved and is precisely what fails in the open
case.

\paragraph{Where the argument stops.}
If $E_n<0$ for infinitely many $n$, then by Proposition~\ref{res:update} the
state $C$ increases at those indices, and no quantity is known to descend.  The
reverse one-sided inequality makes the state nondecreasing, which removes the
stabilisation argument rather than reversing it.  Nothing above excludes that
behaviour.

\section{A finite-horizon residue reduction}\label{sec:residue}

In the surviving case one may normalise the error to a constant negative value,
which is legitimate up to common scaling by Proposition~\ref{res:scale}.  In
that template the next term is forced: at index $n$ its numerator is
\[
 \fnum(n,a)=(n+1)a^{2}-(n+2)a+(n+3),
\]
the
\lword{Erdos243/FiniteHorizonResidue.lean}{22}{forcedNumerator}{forced
numerator}, and the divisor is $n+2$.  The orbit survives one step when the
division is exact, and continues from the quotient.  Formally, put
$\surv(0,i,a)$ true and
\[
 \surv(h+1,i,a)\iff (i+2)\mid\fnum(i,a)
 \ \text{ and }\
 \surv\Bigl(h,\;i+1,\;\tfrac{\fnum(i,a)}{i+2}\Bigr),
\]
the
\lword{Erdos243/FiniteHorizonResidue.lean}{75}{ForcedSurvives}{survival
predicate}.  The template itself is a definition in the formal source, not a
consequence proved there; the derivation that the surviving case takes this
shape is not formalised.

The orbit grows doubly exponentially, so deciding $\surv(h,0,a)$ by iteration
is expensive.  It is unnecessary.

\begin{theorem}[factorial residue reduction]\label{res:residue}
For all $h\in\N$ and $a,b\in\Z$, if $a\equiv b \pmod{(h+1)!}$ then
$\surv(h,0,a)\iff\surv(h,0,b)$.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
Let $M(0,i)=1$ and $M(h+1,i)=(i+2)M(h,i+1)$, so that $M(h,i)$ is an ascending
factorial and $M(h,0)=(h+1)!$.  We show by induction on $h$ that
$a\equiv b\pmod{M(h,i)}$ implies $\surv(h,i,a)\iff\surv(h,i,b)$.  The
numerator is a polynomial with integer coefficients, so the congruence
transfers to $\fnum(i,a)\equiv\fnum(i,b)\pmod{(i+2)M(h,i+1)}$, and reducing the
modulus gives divisibility by $i+2$ for one exactly when for the other.  When
both divisions are exact, cancelling the common factor $i+2$ from the values
and from the modulus leaves
$\fnum(i,a)/(i+2)\equiv\fnum(i,b)/(i+2)\pmod{M(h,i+1)}$, and the inductive
hypothesis applies.
\end{proof}

\noindent Formalised as the
\lword{Erdos243/FiniteHorizonResidue.lean}{134}{forcedSurvives_iff_of_modEq_factorial}{factorial
residue reduction}, on the
\lword{Erdos243/FiniteHorizonResidue.lean}{97}{forcedSurvives_iff_of_modEq}{shrinking-modulus
transport}, the
\lword{Erdos243/FiniteHorizonResidue.lean}{26}{forcedNumerator_modEq}{polynomial
congruence}, and the
\lword{Erdos243/FiniteHorizonResidue.lean}{41}{quotient_modEq_of_modEq_mul}{exact-division
cancellation}; the modulus identifications are the
\lword{Erdos243/FiniteHorizonResidue.lean}{59}{horizonModulus_eq_ascFactorial}{ascending-factorial
form} and the
\lword{Erdos243/FiniteHorizonResidue.lean}{69}{horizonModulus_zero_eq_factorial}{factorial
value at the initial index}.

\paragraph{What the search found.}
Theorem~\ref{res:residue} replaces an orbit of doubly exponential integers by a
residue computation with a shrinking modulus.  Running it over initial states
below $5000$, quotiented by the common scaling of
Proposition~\ref{res:scale}, produced forced prefixes of length $17$ and no
longer; the leaders include the states $1501$, $1723$, $2029$, $2581$, $2971$,
$3571$, $3583$, and $4621$, each of which fails a later divisibility condition.
These are computed values.  A prefix of length $17$ is not evidence that no
infinite orbit exists, and the failure of every tested leader is not a proof
that all leaders fail: arbitrary finite deviations can be followed by an exact
Sylvester tail, so no finite-prefix rule settles the problem.

\section{Complements and further questions}\label{sec:open}

Problem~\#243 is open.  The results above isolate one obligation.

\begin{problem}[the constant-negative orbit]
Show that the divisibility condition sustaining the forced constant-negative
template cannot hold at every index, or exhibit an initial state at which it
does.  By Theorem~\ref{res:residue} the first $h$ conditions depend only on the
state modulo $(h+1)!$, so the question is whether the shrinking-modulus
computation terminates for every residue.
\end{problem}

\begin{problem}[the two published criteria]
Formalise the criterion of Erd\H{o}s and Straus stated in
Section~\ref{sec:problem}, and Duverney's conditional characterisation, under
explicit analytic hypotheses: the weighted recurrence, the tail error estimate,
and the consumer in each case.  Both are cited here and neither is formalised;
each is stronger than every statement in this note.
\end{problem}

\begin{problem}[the critical boundary]
Decide the case in which the normalised undershoot sits at the reciprocal-index
boundary.  The constant-negative template of Section~\ref{sec:residue} lies
exactly there, which is why the available rate hypotheses do not exclude it.
\end{problem}

The formal content changes the shape of the remaining work rather than its
difficulty.  The rigidity step is an identity, and it is available at every
index; the nonnegative case is closed by descent; and what is left is a single
sign condition on an integer sequence, together with the fact that no finite
prefix decides it.
