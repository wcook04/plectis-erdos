\begin{abstract}
We prove that if $E$ satisfies a finite-prime weighted summability
condition at base two and $V$ admits a summable positive divisor cover,
then $\sum_{a\in A}(b^a-1)^{-1}$ is irrational for every infinite
$A\subseteq E\cup V$ and every integer $b\ge2$. The two hereditary
support classes are incomparable, each contains supports of divergent
reciprocal sum, and their unions can lie in neither class. The proof
compares a multiple of the series with an integer and averages the
error first over multiples of a modulus and then over dyadic lengths.
The second average restores the factor $1/a$ that incomplete residue
periods lose, and because both criteria run through the same average,
a single index makes both errors small at once. Irrationality for
arbitrary infinite support at base two remains open.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}\label{sec:problem}
For a set $A$ of positive integers and a real number $b>1$, write
\[
 X_A(b)=\sum_{a\in A}\frac1{b^a-1}.
\]
The series converges by comparison with a geometric series, since
$(b^a-1)^{-1}\le b^{1-a}/(b-1)$. Erd\H{o}s proved that the full
series $X_{\Npos}(b)$ is irrational at every integer base
\cite{erdos1948}, and later obtained the same conclusion for pairwise
coprime supports of finite reciprocal sum \cite[p.~222]{erdos1968}.
On that page he remarked that the coprimality assumption could be
removed, and gave no details. For arbitrary infinite support in base
two the question is Problem~\#257:

\begin{problem}[Erd\H{o}s \#257]\label{res:problem}
Is $X_A(2)$ irrational for every infinite $A\subseteq\Npos$?
\end{problem}

We look for conditions on a support that every infinite subset
inherits. The first replaces reciprocal summability by a weight that
sees divisibility: for $a=2^km$ with $m$ odd, it charges
$1/[m(b^{2^k}-1)]$ where reciprocal summability charges $1/(2^km)$, so
that a large power of $2$ pays for many odd cofactors. More generally,
we fix a finite set $P$ of primes and write
$h(a)=\prod_{p\in P}p^{v_p(a)}$ for the \emph{$P$-part} of $a$, where
$v_p(a)$ is the exponent of $p$ in $a$. The prime powers may grow with
$a$; the set of primes stays fixed.

\begin{theorem}[a weighted condition on the support]
\label{res:weighted-support}
Let $b\ge2$ be an integer, let $A\subseteq\Npos$ be infinite, and let $P$
be a finite nonempty set of primes.  Set
$h(a)=\prod_{p\in P}p^{v_p(a)}$.  If
\begin{equation}\label{eq:weighted-fixed-base}
 W_{b,P}(A):=\sum_{a\in A}
 \frac{h(a)}{a(b^{h(a)}-1)}<\infty,
\end{equation}
then $X_A(b)$ is irrational.  In particular, the base-two condition
\begin{equation*}\label{eq:weighted-return}
 \sum_{a\in A}\frac{h(a)}{a(2^{h(a)}-1)}<\infty
 \tag{W}
\end{equation*}
implies that $X_A(b)$ is irrational for every integer $b\ge2$.  Both
conclusions are hereditary under passage to infinite subsets.
\end{theorem}

For an example beyond reciprocal summability, consider the support
\[
 A_\star=\{2^km:k\ge1,\ m\text{ odd},\ m\le2^{2^k}\}.
\]
For fixed $k$ the odd cofactors have reciprocal sum of order $2^k$, so
the corresponding terms contribute at least $1/4$ to
$\sum_{a\in A_\star}1/a$ and at most $2^{1-k}$
to~\eqref{eq:weighted-return}; Section~\ref{sec:eight-return-extensions}
proves both bounds. Hence every infinite subset of $A_\star$ has an
irrational value at every integer base, although $A_\star$ has divergent
reciprocal sum. Since $h/(2^h-1)\le1$, the theorem also contains the
reciprocal-summable case, and Section~\ref{sec:reciprocal-support}
gives a direct proof of the extension Erd\H{o}s stated.

The second condition uses the joint divisibility of the exponents: it
bounds fractional powers of divisor counts by positive divisor sums
(Theorem~\ref{thm:variable-fractional-cover}). The two conditions are
incomparable, and they combine. If $E$ satisfies the base-two weighted
condition and $V$ has a summable divisor cover, then every infinite
subset of $E\cup V$ has an irrational sum
(Theorem~\ref{res:mixed-supports}). Since two irrational subseries may
add up to a rational number, the proof has to control both errors at
the same index. The separating constructions of
Proposition~\ref{res:weighted-cover-incomparability} produce such a
union that lies outside both individual classes
(Corollary~\ref{res:strict-mixed-supports}). All three criteria are
sufficient conditions; none of them characterises the supports in
Problem~\ref{res:problem}.

Every proof compares a multiple of $X_A(b)$ with an integer. For a
positive integer $N$ we put
\begin{equation}
 \Delta_{b,A}(N)=\sum_{a\in A}\frac{b^{N\bmod a}-1}{b^a-1}
 =(b^N-1)X_A(b)-J_{b,A}(N),\qquad
 J_{b,A}(N)=\sum_{\substack{a\in A\\a\le N}}
             \sum_{j=1}^{\lfloor N/a\rfloor}b^{N-ja}.
 \label{eq:intro-displacement}\tag{D}
\end{equation}
The identity follows on dividing $N$ by each $a$. At an integer base
$J_{b,A}(N)$ is an integer, and for infinite $A$ the displacement
$\Delta_{b,A}(N)$ is positive, because some $a\in A$ exceeds $N$.
Hence, if $X_A(b)=p/q$ with $p\in\mathbb Z$ and $q\ge1$ an integer,
then $q\Delta_{b,A}(N)$ is a positive integer and
$\Delta_{b,A}(N)\ge1/q$. It therefore suffices to find displacements
tending to zero. The displacement is a positive real number and may
exceed $1$, so it should be distinguished from a fractional part;
Erd\H{o}s also proposed working with fractional parts of powers times
the value of the series \cite[p.~226]{erdos1968}.

To make the displacement small we take $N$ to be a multiple of a
modulus. Divisibility then cancels the finitely many exponents that
divide the modulus, and the remaining terms have small means over a
complete period when their gcd with the modulus is large. The bound
for an incomplete period lacks the factor $1/a$, and averaging over
dyadic lengths restores it: an exponent enters the truncated error only
once the sampled range reaches it, and the reciprocals of the later
lengths form a geometric tail bounded by a multiple of $1/a$. Exponents
beyond the sampled range are estimated separately. Because every bound
depends explicitly on the modulus, both criteria can share one average.

Sections~\ref{sec:eight-return-extensions}--\ref{sec:mixed} prove the
three criteria, and
Sections~\ref{sec:comparison}--\ref{sec:map} separate their support
classes and describe the limits of the method. The appendices give the
direct proof for reciprocal-summable supports, the exact denominators
of finite subseries, and tests for whether a prescribed rational number
is a subseries sum; these tests decide neither $1/2$ nor $1/21$.

\section{Finite averages and the weighted criterion}
\label{sec:eight-return-extensions}
Fix the integer base $b$ and the finite prime set $P$. We first
estimate averages over the multiples of a fixed modulus $Q$ and then
choose $Q$ to suit the weighted hypothesis. The role of the gcd is
already visible at $b=2$, $Q=4$ and $a=6$, where the residues of
$4,8,12$ modulo $6$ are $4,2,0$ and
\[
 \frac13\left(\frac{2^4-1}{63}+\frac{2^2-1}{63}+0\right)
 =\frac2{21}\le\frac{2}{6(2^2-1)}=\frac19.
\]
The final expression is the mean of $2^{4m\bmod6}/63$ over the same
period, and if $6\mid Q$ the term with exponent $6$ vanishes at every
multiple of $Q$. The proof uses both effects: divisibility removes
finitely many exponents, and the gcd estimate bounds the rest.

\begin{proof}[Proof of Theorem~\ref{res:weighted-support}]
\emph{The contribution of one exponent.}
Put $d_a(m)=(b^{m\bmod a}-1)/(b^a-1)$ and $g=(Q,a)$, for positive
integers $Q,a,T$. The residues $tQ\bmod a$ run through an orbit of
length $a/g$, and splitting $1,\ldots,T$ into full orbits and a
remainder gives
\begin{equation}\label{eq:weighted-finite-orbit}
 \frac1T\sum_{t=1}^T d_a(tQ)
 \le \frac{g}{a(b^g-1)}+\frac1{T(b^g-1)}.
\end{equation}
Indeed, after we drop the $-1$ in the numerator, each orbit sums to
$\sum_{t=1}^{a/g}b^{tQ\bmod a}/(b^a-1)=1/(b^g-1)$. The complete orbits
give the first term in~\eqref{eq:weighted-finite-orbit}, and by
nonnegativity the remaining indices cost at most one more orbit.
The complete-period term is bounded by the weighted summand of the
theorem whenever $g\ge h(a)$. The modulus chosen below guarantees this
for $h(a)\le H$, and for larger prime parts it gives a uniform lower
bound on $g$ instead. Moreover, for $Y=QT$,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:weighted-outer-short}
 \frac1T\sum_{t=1}^T\sum_{\substack{a\in A\\a>Y}}d_a(tQ)
 \le\frac4T,
\end{equation}
because $d_a(tQ)\le2\,2^{tQ-a}$ when $a>QT$ and
$\sum_{t=1}^T2^{tQ-QT}\le2$.

\emph{Recovering the reciprocal factor.}
For each fixed $a$ the error in~\eqref{eq:weighted-finite-orbit} tends
to zero, but it carries no factor $1/a$, so its sum over the growing
range $a\le QT$ is out of reach. We take $T=2^j$ and sum over $j$
first. A fixed $a$ enters the truncated error only when $2^j\ge a/Q$,
and the reciprocals of those lengths form a geometric tail of sum at
most $2Q/a$. Thus, for integers $Q,M\ge1$ and $\alpha_a\ge0$ with
$\sum_a\alpha_a/a<\infty$,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:weighted-dyadic-short}
 \sum_{j=M}^{2M-1}\frac1{2^j}\sum_{a\le Q2^j}\alpha_a
 \le2Q\sum_{a\ge1}\frac{\alpha_a}{a};
\end{equation}
the estimate remains valid if the dyadic lengths start later. After
division by $M$ the cost $2Q$ becomes $2Q/M$.

\emph{Choosing the modulus.}
Fix $\varepsilon>0$. Choose a finite nonempty $F\subseteq A$ such that
the sum in \eqref{eq:weighted-fixed-base} over $A\setminus F$ is below
$\varepsilon$, and let $L$ be any fixed positive common multiple of
$F$. Besides cancelling $F$, the modulus should contain every power of
a prime in $P$ up to a growing threshold $H$, and we truncate each
prime power separately so that the modulus stays polynomial in $H$.
With $p_*=\max P$ and $H\ge2p_*$, set
\[
 Q=L\prod_{p\in P}p^{\lfloor\log_pH\rfloor},\qquad
 G=\left\lfloor\frac H{p_*}\right\rfloor.
\]
For $a\in F$ the displacement term vanishes, since $a\mid Q$.
For $a\notin F$ with $h(a)\le H$ we have $h(a)\mid Q$, hence
$(Q,a)\ge h(a)$, and since $n/(b^n-1)$ decreases, the complete-period
term in~\eqref{eq:weighted-finite-orbit} is at most
$h(a)/[a(b^{h(a)}-1)]$. These complete-period contributions therefore
sum to less than $\varepsilon$, and the dyadic estimate will control
their incomplete periods.

For $h(a)>H$ we have $(Q,a)\ge G$ instead. Indeed, if every
prime-power component $p^{v_p(a)}$ with $p\in P$ is at most $H$, then
$h(a)\mid Q$ and $(Q,a)\ge h(a)>H$; otherwise some $p^{v_p(a)}>H$
contributes $p^{\lfloor\log_pH\rfloor}>H/p\ge H/p_*\ge G$ to the gcd.
We sum the complete-period bounds over $a\le QT$ using
$\sum_{a\le QT}1/a\le1+\log(QT)$. Since there are at most $QT$
incomplete-period terms, each at most $1/[T(b^G-1)]$, these exponents
contribute at most
\[
 \frac{G(1+\log(QT))+Q}{b^G-1}.
\]
\emph{Balancing the two errors.}
Since $T=2^j$ with $j<2M$, increasing $M$ lowers the
incomplete-period cost $Q/M$ and raises the large-gcd cost $GM/b^G$.
With $F$ and $L$ fixed, $Q\le LH^{|P|}$ is polynomial in $H$, while
$G=H/p_*+O(1)$ grows linearly, so the choice
$M=\lfloor b^{G/2}\rfloor$ sends both costs to zero. Here $M$ counts
the scales $M\le j<2M$, whose observation lengths are $2^j$.
We apply~\eqref{eq:weighted-dyadic-short} with
$\alpha_a={\bf1}_A(a)/(b^{h(a)}-1)$, for which
$\sum_a\alpha_a/a\le W_{b,P}(A)$ because $h(a)\ge1$.
Together with~\eqref{eq:weighted-outer-short}, the preceding estimates give
\begin{equation}\label{eq:weighted-main-bound}
 \frac1M\sum_{j=M}^{2M-1}\frac1{2^j}
 \sum_{t=1}^{2^j}\Delta_{b,A}(tQ)
 \le \varepsilon+\frac{2QW_{b,P}(A)}M
 +\frac{G(1+\log Q+2M\log2)+Q}{b^G-1}+4\,2^{-M}.
\end{equation}
The first two terms account for the complete and incomplete periods of
exponents with $h(a)\le H$, and the last two for the exponents
$a\le QT$ with $h(a)>H$ and for the tail $a>QT$. With $F$ and $L$
fixed, every term after $\varepsilon$ tends to zero as $H\to\infty$.
The left-hand side is the expectation of $\Delta_{b,A}(N)$ when we
choose one of the $M$ scales uniformly and then one of its $2^j$
indices uniformly. Hence, for large $H$, some sampled $N=Qt$ satisfies
$\Delta_{b,A}(N)<2\varepsilon$, and since $\varepsilon$ is arbitrary
this contradicts the lower bound $1/q$ forced by rationality. The
argument gives no rate of decay in $N$. Finally,
$W_{b,P}(A)\le W_{2,P}(A)$ for $b\ge2$, and the weighted sum can only
decrease when we pass to a subset.
\end{proof}

Duverney and Tachiya~\cite[Section~2, (2.3)--(2.9)]{duverneytachiya}
also bound an average along an arithmetic progression and select an
index at which a combined quantity is small; their progression is built
by the Chinese remainder theorem. In the proof above, the additional
dyadic average is what controls the incomplete-period error while the
modulus grows.


\subsection{An example beyond reciprocal summability}
For the support from the introduction we compare the two sums layer by
layer. Recall that
\[
 A_\star=\{2^k m:k\ge1,\ m\text{ odd},\ m\le2^{2^k}\}.
\]
Elements of different layers have different $2$-adic valuations, so the
layers are disjoint. For $r\ge2$ put
\[
 S_r=\sum_{\substack{m\le2^r\\m\text{ odd}}}\frac1m.
\]
Each block $2^j\le m<2^{j+1}$ with $1\le j<r$ contains $2^{j-1}$ odd
integers, whose reciprocals sum to between $1/4$ and $1/2$, and
including $m=1$ we obtain $r/4\le S_r\le r$. The reciprocals in the
$k$th layer therefore sum to $2^{-k}S_{2^k}\ge1/4$, and
$\sum_{a\in A_\star}1/a$ diverges. For $P=\{2\}$ the same layer
contributes to the weighted sum at most
\[
 \frac{S_{2^k}}{2^{2^k}-1}
 \le\frac{2^k}{2^{2^k}-1}
 \le2^{1-k}.
\]
The last bound uses $2^{2^k}-1\ge2^{2^k-1}$ and $2^k\ge2k$, and
summing over $k$ proves~\eqref{eq:weighted-return}, although the
reciprocal sum diverges.


One can also prescribe the sets of primes for which the weighted sum
converges. Let $E$ be a finite set of primes and let $\mathcal U$ be
an upward-closed family of subsets of $E$ that contains $E$ and omits
$\varnothing$. A finite union of constructions of the kind just
described has divergent reciprocal sum, and for every $b\ge2$ and every
finite prime set $P$ its weighted sum $W_{b,P}$ is finite exactly when
$P\cap E\in\mathcal U$. Taking $P=E$, we see that every infinite subset
has an irrational sum at every integer base. The construction is given
in
\papersectionlink{erdos257-mersenne-reasoning-surface.pdf}{record257:witness-rules}
{Section~1.3 of the companion paper}.

\section{Positive divisor majorants}\label{sec:common-kernel}
The weighted criterion treats the exponents one at a time, whereas a
divisor cover uses their joint divisibility. To describe it we write
the series in terms of its divisor counts:
\begin{equation}
 c_A(n)=\#\{a\in A:a\mid n\},\qquad
 X_A(b)=\sum_{n\ge1}c_A(n)b^{-n}.
 \label{eq:incidence}
\end{equation}
This follows on expanding $(b^a-1)^{-1}=\sum_{j\ge1}b^{-aj}$ and
interchanging the nonnegative sums. The selector $\mathbf1_A$ takes
only the values zero and one, whereas $0\le c_A(n)\le\tau(n)$, where
$\tau(n)$ is the number of positive divisors of $n$. The $c_A(n)$ are
power-series coefficients before any carrying, and they may exceed the
range of base-$b$ digits.

The criteria of Kaneko, Suzuki and Tachiya
\cite[Theorems~1 and~3]{kanekosuzukitachiya} require the nonzero
power-series coefficients to be sparse, and sparsity of the selected
Lambert denominators does not supply this. For any nonempty $A$, the
coefficient $c_A(n)$ is positive on every multiple of $\min A$, so its
nonzero positions have positive lower density and violate their
support-counting hypotheses. Their criteria therefore do not apply
directly to~\eqref{eq:incidence}. The density calculation, and the
difference between their remote-tail average and our displacement,
appear in
\papersectionlink{erdos257-mersenne-reasoning-surface.pdf}{record257:weighted-proof}
{Section~1.2 of the companion paper}.


\subsection{A finite divisor set and the cover condition}
For finite $F\subseteq\Npos$ write $f_F(n)=\#\{a\in F:a\mid n\}$, and
consider the four exponents $F=\{2,6,10,30\}$. The count vanishes when
$n$ is odd, and for even $n$ each of the primes $3$ and $5$ that
divides $n$ doubles it. Thus
\[
 f_F(n)=\mathbf1_{2\mid n}(1+\mathbf1_{3\mid n})(1+\mathbf1_{5\mid n}).
\]
For $0<\alpha\le1$ put $z=2^\alpha-1$. Raising to the power $\alpha$
and expanding, we obtain a positive divisor sum:
\[
 f_F(n)^\alpha
 =\mathbf1_{2\mid n}+z\mathbf1_{6\mid n}
  +z\mathbf1_{10\mid n}+z^2\mathbf1_{30\mid n},\qquad
 C=\frac12\left(1+\frac z3\right)\left(1+\frac z5\right).
\]
Since $\mathbf1_{d\mid n}$ has mean $1/d$, the constant $C$ is the mean
of this expansion, and the same factorisation applies to
$\{qd:d\mid\prod_{p\in P}p\}$ whenever no prime in $P$ divides $q$.
A small exponent $\alpha$ shrinks the coefficients, but the tail
estimate then costs $(2^\alpha-1)^{-1}=1/z$. The condition below
balances these two effects over a sequence of finite sets.

\begin{theorem}[a summable divisor-cover criterion]\label{thm:variable-fractional-cover}
For each $j\ge1$, let $F_j\subseteq\Npos$ be finite, let
$0<\alpha_j\le1$, and let $c_{j,d}\ge0$ satisfy
\[
 f_{F_j}(n)^{\alpha_j}\le\sum_{d\mid n}c_{j,d}\quad(n\ge1).
\]
Set $C_j=\sum_{d\ge1}c_{j,d}/d$.  If
\begin{equation*}
 \sum_{j\ge1}\frac{C_j2^{j\alpha_j}}{2^{\alpha_j}-1}<\infty,
 \tag{V}\label{eq:strengthened-cover}
\end{equation*}
then $X_A(b)$ is irrational for every infinite
$A\subseteq\bigcup_jF_j$ and every integer $b\ge2$.
\end{theorem}

For example, take $F_j=\{4^j\}$, $\alpha_j=1$ and $c_{j,4^j}=1$,
with all other coefficients zero. Then $C_j=4^{-j}$, and the cost
in~\eqref{eq:strengthened-cover} is $\sum_j2^{-j}$. Every finite set
admits the majorant $\alpha_j=1$, $c_{j,d}=\mathbf1_{F_j}(d)$, so the
whole restriction lies in the summability of the total cost. The
covering sets may overlap, and their majorants may use divisors outside
them. Section~\ref{sec:comparison} gives a necessary condition that
holds whatever these choices are.

\subsection{A finite mean for shifted divisor tails}
A divisor majorant turns the tail into a sum of geometric tails, one
for each divisor. Indeed, the positive offsets $r$ with $d\mid N+r$ are
$d-(N\bmod d),2d-(N\bmod d),\ldots$, so that
\[
 \sum_{\substack{r\ge1\\d\mid N+r}}B^{-r}
 =\frac{B^{N\bmod d}}{B^d-1}.
\]
Fractional powers introduce bases $B=2^\alpha$ that may be close to
$1$. The estimate we need must therefore stay uniform after
multiplication by $B-1$, and it must allow the modulus to grow, since
the mixed proof fixes the first covering sets before it enlarges the
modulus.

For $1<B\le2$, positive integers $L,d,M$, and an integer $R\ge0$, put
\[
 w_{B,d}(n)=\frac{B^{n\bmod d}}{B^d-1},\qquad
 \mathscr D_{L;R,M}F
 =\frac1M\sum_{k=R}^{R+M-1}\frac1{2^k}
      \sum_{m=1}^{2^k}F(Lm).
\]
In words, we choose $k$ uniformly from $R,\ldots,R+M-1$, then $m$
uniformly from $1,\ldots,2^k$, and observe $N=Lm$. Each scale receives
the same weight, and an index $N$ observed at several scales collects
the sum of their weights.

The finite estimate
\begin{equation*}
 \mathscr D_{L;R,M}w_{B,d}
 \le\frac{1+4L/M}{d(B-1)}
 \tag{S}\label{eq:mixed-finite-kernel}
\end{equation*}
is uniform as $B\downarrow1$ after multiplication by $B-1$, where it
becomes $(1+4L/M)/d$. The factor $(B-1)^{-1}$ is the source of the
cost $(2^\alpha-1)^{-1}$ in \eqref{eq:strengthened-cover}. The bound
holds for every starting scale $R$ and depends on the modulus only
through the ratio $L/M$, which tends to zero on the scales used in the
weighted proof.

To prove~\eqref{eq:mixed-finite-kernel}, fix $T=2^k$ and compare $d$
with the largest sampled multiple $LT$. When $d\le LT$, the orbit of
$L$ modulo $d$ has length $d/g$, with $g=(L,d)$, and total weight
$1/(B^g-1)$. Splitting the sample into complete cycles (possibly none)
and one remaining piece, we obtain
\[
 \frac1T\sum_{m=1}^T w_{B,d}(Lm)
 \le\frac{g}{d(B^g-1)}+\frac1{T(B^g-1)}
 \le\frac1{d(B-1)}+\frac1{T(B-1)}.
\]
Over the dyadic lengths with $d\le L2^k$, the errors $1/[T(B-1)]$ sum
to at most $2L/[d(B-1)]$. When $d>2LT$, we have $Lm\bmod d=Lm$ and
$2Lm\le d-1$, hence
$\sum_{i=0}^{d-1}B^i\ge dB^{(d-1)/2}\ge dB^{Lm}$, and each term
$w_{B,d}(Lm)$ is at most $1/[d(B-1)]$. For fixed $d$, the transition
range $LT<d\le2LT$ occurs for at most one $T$ as $T$ doubles, and at
that scale geometric summation gives
\[
 \frac1T\sum_{m=1}^T w_{B,d}(Lm)
 =\frac{B^L}{T(B^L-1)}\frac{B^{LT}-1}{B^d-1}
 \le\frac{LB^L}{d(B^L-1)}
 \le\frac{2L}{d(B-1)}.
\]
In the first inequality, the convexity of $x\mapsto B^x-1$ and its
value $0$ at the origin bound the ratio by $LT/d$; the last uses
$(B^L-1)/(B-1)=\sum_{i=0}^{L-1}B^i\ge B^{L-1}$ and $B\le2$. Before
division by $M$, the incomplete periods and the possible transition
scale together cost at most $4L/[d(B-1)]$, and adding the mean term
$1/[d(B-1)]$ proves~\eqref{eq:mixed-finite-kernel}. Since all terms
are nonnegative, we may
sum the estimate against any coefficients $c_d$ with
$\sum_dc_d/d<\infty$.

\subsection{Proof of the cover criterion}
\begin{proof}[Proof of Theorem~\ref{thm:variable-fractional-cover}]
Write $B_j=2^{\alpha_j}$ and
\[
 U_j(N)=\sum_{r\ge1}2^{-r}f_{F_j}(N+r),\qquad
 V_j(N)=\sum_{d\ge1}c_{j,d}w_{B_j,d}(N).
\]
We must make the tails $U_j$ small, and~\eqref{eq:mixed-finite-kernel}
controls the means of the $V_j$. Subadditivity of $x\mapsto x^{\alpha_j}$
and the divisor majorant connect the two:
\[
 U_j(N)^{\alpha_j}
 \le\sum_{r\ge1}B_j^{-r}f_{F_j}(N+r)^{\alpha_j}
 \le\sum_{d\ge1}c_{j,d}
       \sum_{\substack{r\ge1\\d\mid N+r}}B_j^{-r}
 =V_j(N).
\]
For the displacement itself, the geometric-series identity gives
\[
 \Delta_{2,F_j}(N)=U_j(N)-X_{F_j}(2)\le U_j(N).
\]
For $j\le J$ divisibility will cancel the displacement, so that
$U_j(N)=X_{F_j}(2)$, and only the tails with $j>J$ have to be made
small.

Fix $\varepsilon>0$ and allot the allowance $t_j=\varepsilon2^{-j}$ to
the $j$th tail, so that the allowances sum to $\varepsilon$. Since
$t_j^{-\alpha_j}V_j<1$ implies $U_j<t_j$, this allocation accounts for
the factor $2^{j\alpha_j}$ in the cover cost. Choose $J$ with
\[
 K_J:=\sum_{j>J}\frac{C_jt_j^{-\alpha_j}}{B_j-1}<\frac14.
\]
which is possible because $\varepsilon^{-\alpha_j}\le\max(1,\varepsilon^{-1})$.
Choose $L$ divisible by every member of the first $J$ finite sets.
For every $R\ge0$ and $M\ge4L$, the estimate~\eqref{eq:mixed-finite-kernel}
bounds the $\mathscr D_{L;R,M}$-mean of
$S_J(N):=\sum_{j>J}t_j^{-\alpha_j}V_j(N)$ by $(1+4L/M)K_J<1/2$, so
some sample satisfies $S_J(N)<1$. This single choice controls all
countably many tails at once: each nonnegative summand is below $1$,
hence $U_j(N)<t_j$ for every $j>J$. The first $J$ sets contribute no
displacement, the later sets cover the rest of $A$, and overlaps only
enlarge the upper bound. Thus
\[
 0<\Delta_{2,A}(N)\le\sum_{j>J}U_j(N)\le\varepsilon.
\]
Equation~\eqref{eq:intro-displacement} therefore excludes rationality
at base two. To pass to larger bases, note that for $0\le r<d$ the function
$(b^r-1)/(b^d-1)$ is nonincreasing on $b>1$. For $r>0$ we cancel $b-1$
and write it as $A(b)/(A(b)+C(b))$, where $A(b)=\sum_{i<r}b^i$ and
$C(b)=\sum_{r\le j<d}b^j$; its derivative has the sign of
$A'C-AC'=\sum_{i<r\le j<d}(i-j)b^{i+j-1}<0$. Hence
$\Delta_{b,A}(N)\le\Delta_{2,A}(N)$ for every real $b\ge2$, and when
$b$ is an integer this contradicts the lower bound that
\eqref{eq:intro-displacement} gives under rationality. Since any
prescribed positive integer may be included as a divisor of $L$, the
chosen indices can also be taken arbitrarily large.
\end{proof}

The same proof permits any positive weights $\eta_j$ with
$\sum_j\eta_j=1$: we replace $2^{j\alpha_j}$
in~\eqref{eq:strengthened-cover} by $\eta_j^{-\alpha_j}$ and take
$t_j=\varepsilon\eta_j$. These weights allocate the error allowances
and leave the Lambert subseries itself unweighted. The stronger
condition
$\sum_jC_j2^{j\alpha_j}2^{\alpha_j}/(2^{\alpha_j}-1)^2<\infty$
implies~\eqref{eq:strengthened-cover}, since each of its summands is
the corresponding summand of~\eqref{eq:strengthened-cover} multiplied
by $2^{\alpha_j}/(2^{\alpha_j}-1)>1$. That argument compares the two
hypotheses for a given cover. After optimisation over all covers, the
inclusion of the corresponding support classes is strict, as a
\href{https://github.com/wcook04/plectis-erdos/blob/3973d3b10bce72017b8f60093f0d6c3f4f592d80/lean/ErdosProblems/Erdos257/PaperCompleteR8/AnalyticIncomparability.lean\#L41}{Lean proof}
shows with an explicit infinite squarefree support.

\section{A common index for the two criteria}\label{sec:mixed}
Small displacements at unrelated indices give no control of the union,
since the two errors must be small at the same index. We therefore put
both finite cancellation requirements into one modulus and bound both
remaining errors on the same finite distribution. The weighted proof
enlarges the modulus, and (S) keeps the cover estimate valid while it
does so. We work at base two and pass to larger integer bases by
comparing displacements.
\begin{samepage}
\begin{theorem}[mixed weighted and cover supports]\label{res:mixed-supports}
Let $E,V\subseteq\Npos$.  Suppose $E$ satisfies
\eqref{eq:weighted-return} for a finite nonempty prime set $P$, and
$V\subseteq\bigcup_jF_j$ for finite sets and nonnegative majorants
satisfying the hypotheses of Theorem~\ref{thm:variable-fractional-cover},
with either~\eqref{eq:strengthened-cover} or its positive-weight variant.
Then $X_A(b)$ is irrational for every
infinite $A\subseteq E\cup V$ and every integer $b\ge2$.
\end{theorem}
\end{samepage}
\begin{proof}
\emph{Fix the finite cancellation requirements.}
Fix $\varepsilon>0$ and an integer $N_0\ge1$, and put $\rho=\varepsilon/3$.
Use the cover notation $B_j,U_j,V_j$ above, with $\eta_j=2^{-j}$ in
the case~\eqref{eq:strengthened-cover}.  Set $t_j=\rho\eta_j$ and choose
$J$ so that
\[
 K_J=\sum_{j>J}\frac{C_jt_j^{-\alpha_j}}{B_j-1}<\frac1{16}.
\]
Choose a finite $F\subseteq E$ so that the weighted sum over $E\setminus F$ is
$\kappa<\rho/16$. Let $L$ be a positive common multiple of $N_0$, of
the members of $F$, and of the members of the first $J$ finite sets.
We fix $J$, $F$ and $L$ before choosing any scales, and every later
modulus will be a multiple of $L$.

\emph{Estimate both errors on one distribution.}
With these finite data fixed, we use the base-two choices
\[
 Q=L\prod_{p\in P}p^{\lfloor\log_pH\rfloor},\qquad
 G=\lfloor H/\max P\rfloor,\qquad M=\lfloor2^{G/2}\rfloor.
\]
Both estimates use $\mathscr D_{Q;M,M}$, in which the scale index $k$
runs from $M$ to $2M-1$ and each observation is $N=Qm$. The
estimate~\eqref{eq:weighted-main-bound} allows any common multiple of
$F$ as $L$, so it accommodates the extra divisors required by the
cover. It also holds for finite or empty $E$, since infinitude entered
the weighted proof only through the final positive displacement.
Consequently
\[
 \mathscr D_{Q;M,M}(\Delta_{2,E}/\rho)<\frac18
\]
for sufficiently large $H$. On the same finite distribution, (S) and
the interchange of nonnegative sums give
\[
 \mathscr D_{Q;M,M}S_J\le(1+4Q/M)K_J<\frac18,
 \qquad S_J=\sum_{j>J}t_j^{-\alpha_j}V_j,
\]
because $Q/M\to0$.

\emph{Choose the index only after adding the errors.}
Adding the two estimates, we obtain
\[
 \mathscr D_{Q;M,M}\bigl(\Delta_{2,E}/\rho+S_J\bigr)<\frac14.
\]
This step uses only the linearity of the mean, and requires no
independence between the two errors. We choose a sampled $N=Qm$ at
which the nonnegative sum is less than $1$. Then
$\Delta_{2,E}(N)<\rho$ and every summand of $S_J(N)$ is less than $1$,
so $U_j^{\alpha_j}\le V_j$ gives $U_j(N)<t_j$ for every $j>J$. The
displacement terms from the first $J$ finite sets vanish, because their
exponents divide $L$ and hence $N$. Therefore
\[
 \Delta_{2,A}(N)\le\Delta_{2,E}(N)+\Delta_{2,V}(N)<2\rho<\varepsilon,
 \qquad N\ge Q\ge L\ge N_0.
\]
The inequality remains valid when $E$ and $V$ overlap, and
$\Delta_{2,A}(N)>0$ because $A$ is infinite. By the comparison
$0<\Delta_{b,A}(N)\le\Delta_{2,A}(N)$ from the proof of
Theorem~\ref{thm:variable-fractional-cover}, the displacements take
arbitrarily small positive values at every integer base, and
\eqref{eq:intro-displacement} rules out rationality.
\end{proof}
\section{Comparison of the support classes}\label{sec:comparison}
Let $\mathcal W_b$ consist of supports satisfying the weighted condition
at base $b$ for some finite nonempty prime set, and let $\mathcal C$
consist of supports with a cover satisfying~\eqref{eq:strengthened-cover}.
Membership asks for a single finite prime set, or a single summable
cover, that serves the whole support, and the constructions below defeat
every such choice.

\begin{proposition}[incomparable support criteria]
\label{res:weighted-cover-incomparability}
There are infinite positive supports $E$ and $V$ such that
\[
 E\in\mathcal W_2,\quad E\notin\mathcal C,\qquad
 V\in\mathcal C,\quad V\notin\mathcal W_b\ (b\ge2),\qquad
 \sum_{a\in V}\frac1a=\infty.
\]
\end{proposition}

Both constructions are built from blocks $\{qd:d\mid\prod_{p\in P}p\}$,
arranged with opposite divisibility patterns. In $E$, the growing
powers $q=2^k$ make the weighted sum converge, while the many
simultaneous divisors defeat every cover. In $V$, pairwise disjoint
prime sets force every finite-prime weighted sum to diverge, while
fractional powers of the divisor counts keep the cover cost summable.

\subsection{A lower bound independent of the cover}
To exclude every cover at once, we bound the cost of an arbitrary cover
from below in terms of finite subsets of the support. For finite $F$
let $\mathbb E_F$ denote the uniform mean modulo
$\operatorname{lcm}(F)$, with $\operatorname{lcm}(\varnothing)=1$ and
$f_{\varnothing}=0$. We allow arbitrary weights $\eta_j>0$ with
$\sum_j\eta_j=1$, so that the lower bound also applies to reweighted
covers. Set
\[
 K=\sum_j\frac{C_j\eta_j^{-\alpha_j}}{2^{\alpha_j}-1},\qquad
 \Psi(0)=0,\quad
 \Psi(t)=\inf_{0<\alpha\le1}\frac{t^\alpha}{2^\alpha-1}\quad(t\ge1).
\]
Here $K$ depends on the cover together with its majorants, exponents
and weights, whereas $\Psi$ minimises over the scalar exponent $\alpha$
alone. Writing $\log^+t=\log\max\{1,t\}$, we claim that for every
finite $F\subseteq\bigcup_jF_j$,
\begin{equation}
 K\ge\mathbb E_F\Psi(f_F)\ge e\,\mathbb E_F\log^+f_F.
 \label{eq:cover-log-obstruction}
\end{equation}
Indeed, if $f_F(n)=t>0$, coverage gives $\sum_jf_{F_j}(n)\ge t$, so
some $j$ satisfies $f_{F_j}(n)\ge\eta_jt$, and multiplying its divisor
majorant by $\eta_j^{-\alpha_j}/(2^{\alpha_j}-1)$ gives at least
$t^{\alpha_j}/(2^{\alpha_j}-1)\ge\Psi(t)$. The index $j$ may depend on
$n$, but the sum of all these weighted majorants bounds
$\Psi(f_F(n))$ at every $n$. We average over $1\le n\le X$. Each
divisor indicator has mean $\lfloor X/d\rfloor/X\le1/d$, so the
weighted majorants have total mean at most $K$, and as $X\to\infty$
the mean of $\Psi(f_F)$ tends to $\mathbb E_F\Psi(f_F)$. This proves
the first inequality; only the finitely supported function $f_F$ needs
a period, and the covering moduli need not divide
$\operatorname{lcm}(F)$. The second inequality is immediate for
$t=0,1$, and for $t>1$ it follows from $2^\alpha-1\le\alpha$ and
$e^u/u\ge e$ with $u=\alpha\log t$.

For the blocks $\{qd:d\mid\prod_{p\in P}p\}$,
Appendix~\ref{app:elementary-details} determines the optimal cost
asymptotically, in~\eqref{eq:optimal-cube-cost}; the
separation below needs only~\eqref{eq:cover-log-obstruction}.

\subsection{Two separating constructions}
\begin{proof}[Proof of Proposition~\ref{res:weighted-cover-incomparability}]
Both constructions use the divergence of the reciprocal sum over odd
primes, which persists after finitely many primes are removed. If it
converged, $\prod_{p\text{ odd}}(1+1/p)$ would bound the reciprocal sum
of the odd squarefree integers, and the decomposition $n=ds^2$ would
then make the odd harmonic series converge.

\emph{A weighted support with no divisor cover.}
The $k$th group of exponents will carry the factor $2^k$. Since the
event $v_2(n)=k$ has density $2^{-(k+1)}$, a prime reciprocal sum of
order $2^k$ contributes a fixed amount to the mean logarithmic count,
and taking its leading constant below $\log2$ leaves exponential decay
in the weighted sum. Accordingly, choose pairwise disjoint finite sets
$P_k$ of odd primes such that
$2^k/4\le S_k:=\sum_{p\in P_k}1/p<2^k/4+1$.  Put
$M_k=\prod_{p\in P_k}p$ and
$E=\bigcup_{k\ge1}\{2^kd:d\mid M_k\}$.
For $P=\{2\}$ the weighted sum is
\[
 \sum_{k\ge1}\frac{\prod_{p\in P_k}(1+1/p)}{2^{2^k}-1}
 \le 2e\sum_{k\ge1}
       \exp\bigl(-(\log2-1/4)2^k\bigr)<\infty.
\]
For $F_m=\bigcup_{k=1}^m\{2^kd:d\mid M_k\}$ we average
$\log^+f_{F_m}$ over a period, splitting along the disjoint events
$v_2(n)=k<m$ of density $2^{-(k+1)}$. On the $k$th event we use only
the $k$th group, which gives $f_{F_m}(n)\ge2^{Z_k(n)}$ with
$Z_k(n)=\sum_{p\in P_k}\mathbf1_{p\mid n}$. Since divisibility by odd
primes is independent of that event,
\[
 \mathbb E_{F_m}\log^+f_{F_m}
 \ge(\log2)\sum_{k=1}^{m-1}2^{-(k+1)}S_k
 \ge\frac{m-1}{8}\log2.
\]
By~\eqref{eq:cover-log-obstruction}, a cover
satisfying~\eqref{eq:strengthened-cover} would bound all these means by
its fixed cost, which is impossible since they grow with $m$. Thus
$E\notin\mathcal C$.

\emph{A divisor cover with no finite-prime weighted condition.}
Inductively choose odd primes $q_j\ge4^j$ and pairwise disjoint finite
sets $P_j$ of odd primes, each time using only primes not used before.
We stop each $P_j$ at the first prefix with
$R_j:=\prod_{p\in P_j}(1+1/p)\ge q_j$; the divergence of the prime
reciprocal sum permits this, and minimality gives $q_j\le R_j<4q_j/3$.
Put $S_j=\sum_{p\in P_j}1/p$, $M_j=\prod_{p\in P_j}p$, and
$V=\bigcup_{j\ge1}F_j$, where $F_j=\{q_jd:d\mid M_j\}$. Each prime
divides exponents in at most one of the sets $F_j$, and
\[
 \sum_{a\in F_j}\frac1a=\frac{R_j}{q_j}\in[1,4/3).
\]
Hence $\sum_{a\in V}1/a$ diverges. Given a finite set $P$ of primes,
every exponent in all but finitely many of the sets $F_j$ is coprime
to $\prod_{p\in P}p$. On each such $F_j$ we have $h_P(a)=1$, so $F_j$
contributes $R_j/[q_j(b-1)]\ge1/(b-1)$ to the base-$b$ weighted sum.
Hence $V\notin\mathcal W_b$ for every integer $b\ge2$.

It remains to make the whole cover cost summable. The inequalities
$\log(1+x)\ge x-x^2/2$ and $\sum_{p\in P_j}p^{-2}\le1$ give
$S_j\le\log R_j+1/2<\log q_j+1$. Writing $z_j=2^{\alpha_j}-1$, we see
that the index weight and the divisor product contribute
$(1+z_j)^j\prod_{p\in P_j}(1+z_j/p)\le\exp(z_j(j+S_j))$, and we choose
$z_j(j+S_j)=1$ to keep this factor bounded at the price of the
remaining cost $1/(q_jz_j)$. Set
\[
 z_j=(j+S_j)^{-1},\qquad
 \alpha_j=\log_2(1+z_j),\qquad
 c_{j,q_jd}=z_j^{\omega(d)}\quad(d\mid M_j),
\]
with all other coefficients zero, where $\omega(d)$ is the number of
prime factors of $d$. If $q_j\mid n$ and exactly $Z$ primes of $P_j$
divide $n$, the divisor majorant equals
$(1+z_j)^Z=f_{F_j}(n)^{\alpha_j}$, and if $q_j\nmid n$ both sides
vanish. The sum defining $C_j$ is
$C_j=q_j^{-1}\prod_{p\in P_j}(1+z_j/p)$, whence
\[
 \frac{C_j2^{j\alpha_j}}{2^{\alpha_j}-1}
 =\frac{(1+z_j)^j}{q_jz_j}
    \prod_{p\in P_j}(1+z_j/p)
 \le\frac{e(j+S_j)}{q_j}
 \le e\bigl((1+\log4)j+1\bigr)4^{-j}.
\]
The last step uses the fact that $(j+\log x+1)/x$ decreases for
$x\ge4^j$. The bound is summable in $j$ and already includes the
factor $2^{j\alpha_j}$ required by~\eqref{eq:strengthened-cover}, so
$V\in\mathcal C$.
\end{proof}


\subsection{A strict enlargement and finite unions}
The mixed theorem therefore applies to a support that lies outside both
individual classes.
\begin{corollary}[a support requiring the mixed criterion]
\label{res:strict-mixed-supports}
There is an infinite positive support $U$ with
$U\notin\mathcal C$ and $U\notin\mathcal W_b$ for every integer $b\ge2$,
such that $X_A(b)$ is irrational for every infinite $A\subseteq U$
and every integer $b\ge2$.
\end{corollary}
\begin{proof}
Let $U=E\cup V$, with $E$ and $V$ as in
Proposition~\ref{res:weighted-cover-incomparability}. A cover of $U$
is also a cover of $E$, and since all summands are nonnegative,
convergence of the weighted sum over $U$ for some prime set would give
convergence of the sum over $V$ for the same prime set. Thus $U$
belongs to neither individual class, and Theorem~\ref{res:mixed-supports}
gives irrationality for every infinite subset of $U$ at every integer
base $b\ge2$.
\end{proof}
The cover class defined by (V), with its fixed dyadic weights, is
closed under finite unions. When two covers are interleaved, the
index-dependent factor $2^{j\alpha_j}$ has to be controlled, and we do
this by placing their $j$th finite sets at positions $2j-1$ and $2j$
and halving each exponent, which keeps the factor from increasing. The
old majorant coefficients still work, since $f^{\alpha/2}\le f^\alpha$
for every nonnegative integer $f$, and it remains to compare the
denominators in the cost. If $T_j=C_j2^{j\alpha_j}/(2^{\alpha_j}-1)$
denotes the original $j$th cost, the new cost is at most
\[
 \frac{C_j2^{j\alpha_j}}{2^{\alpha_j/2}-1}
 =(1+2^{\alpha_j/2})T_j\le(1+\sqrt2)T_j.
\]
For $r$ covers we interleave at positions at most $rj$ and divide each
exponent by $r$, and the cost ratio is then
$(2^\alpha-1)/(2^{\alpha/r}-1)<2r$. Weighted supports are closed under
finite unions as well: we take the union of their prime sets, which can
only increase $h_P(a)$, and $h/(b^h-1)$ decreases with $h$. Since both
criteria are hereditary and admit finite supports, the mixed class is
closed under finite unions and finite changes, already with the fixed
dyadic weights in (V). Countable unions may leave the mixed class:
every prime singleton is admitted, yet the full set of primes fails the
small-displacement condition, as Section~\ref{sec:map} shows. Finally,
the reciprocal-summable class is contained in the weighted class, since
$h/(2^h-1)\le1$, and Appendix~\ref{sec:reciprocal-support} gives a
direct proof for it.

\section{Limits of the small-displacement criterion}\label{sec:map}
Small displacement is sufficient for irrationality, and the full
support already shows that it is far from necessary:
\[
 \Delta_{2,\Npos}(N)
 >(2^N-1)\sum_{a>N}2^{-a}=1-2^{-N}\ge\frac12
 \qquad(N\ge1).
\]
Erd\H{o}s nevertheless proved the full-support value
\mword{Erdos249257/CertificateKernel.lean}{8328}{irrational_erdosSum_full_support}{irrational}~\cite{erdos1948}.
A proof for arbitrary support therefore cannot rely on making these
displacements tend to zero in every case, although other uses of the
displacement, integer linear forms and averaging arguments remain
available.

The set $\mathcal P$ of all primes gives a second obstruction. Every
prime singleton is admitted by both support criteria, and yet
$\Delta_{2,\mathcal P}(N)>1/3$ for every $N\ge1$. Indeed, writing
$\omega(n)$ for the number of distinct prime divisors of $n$, we have
$\sum_{r\ge1}2^{-r}\omega(N+r)\ge1$, whereas
$X_{\mathcal P}(2)\le\sum_{a\ge2}(2^a-1)^{-1}<2/3$.


Tao and Ter\"av\"ainen nevertheless prove that $X_{\mathcal P}(2)$ is
irrational \cite[Theorem~1.3, p.~4]{taoteravainen2025}. The paragraph
after their theorem states extensions to prime support at every
integer base and to the full set of prime powers at base~$2$, with the
modifications left to the reader; arbitrary infinite sets of primes lie
outside its scope. No theorem here assumes such an extension.


The logarithmic lower bound~\eqref{eq:cover-log-obstruction} has no
converse of the obvious kind: a logarithmic majorant in place of a
fractional one need not control averages along the multiples of a
prescribed modulus. The counterexample, and the bound that survives on
ordinary initial intervals, are proved in
\papersectionlink{erdos257-mersenne-reasoning-surface.pdf}{record257:logarithmic-sampling}
{Section~13 of the companion paper}; those proofs were first worked
out in an AI-assisted note of 17 September 2026. They concern finite
averages only, and leave open an irrationality criterion built on other
uses of logarithmic divisor counts.


For squarefree support, irrationality already follows from the theorem
of Duverney and Tachiya, at every base $2^j$ with $j\ge1$, by their
Corollary~1.2 and Example~1.1~\cite[p.~4]{duverneytachiya}. Their
corollary covers the $s$-free products of any pairwise coprime sequence
of polynomial growth, and they present it as support for the
conjecture of Erd\H{o}s and Graham stated here as
Problem~\ref{res:problem}. For the squarefree support with $1$ removed,
the divisor count is $2^{\omega(n)}-1$, which is odd for $n\ge2$; this
parity excludes a first-block-divisibility condition at even bases.
Adjoining $1$ removes the parity obstruction and changes the sum by a
rational number, so the obstruction concerns the normalisation of the
certificate and says nothing against irrationality of the sum. Details,
and the other known support families, appear in
\papersectionlink{erdos257-mersenne-reasoning-surface.pdf}{record257:known-supports}
{the comparison with known support theorems in the companion paper}.

A criterion based on growth alone gives a different class: if
$A=\{c_1<c_2<\cdots\}$ and $\limsup_n c_n/2^n=\infty$, then $X_A(b)$
is irrational for every integer $b\ge2$ \cite[Theorem~1]{erdos1975}.
The same section of the companion paper derives this from Erd\H{o}s's
criterion on denominator growth and gives examples showing that this
class and reciprocal summability are incomparable. The interval-filling
constructions of~\cite{bkkkz2026} and the freely chosen lacunary
denominators of~\cite{vandoornkovac} concern sequences other than
subseries of the fixed Mersenne weights.

Arguments based on prime incidences need further care: multiplying all
prime exponents by $2$ creates positive correlations between their
divisibility indicators, and size conditions alone cannot restore the
independence. The identity and the covariance calculation appear at the
end of
\papersectionlink{erdos257-mersenne-reasoning-surface.pdf}{record257:mixed-proof}
{the companion paper's discussion of mixed supports}.

\subsection{Algebraic bases on separated supports}
\label{sec:separated-support-transfer}
The companion paper
\href{optimal-sparse-perturbations.pdf}{\emph{Reading eight Erd\H{o}s
problems together}}, in its subsection ``Divisibility cuts at every
algebraic base'', states a transcendence extension based on the
number-field Subspace Theorem. Its hypothesis is an infinite set $H$
with indices $L_j<M_j$, where $L_j\to\infty$ and $M_j-L_j\to\infty$,
such that every $n\in H$ with $n\le L_j$ divides $L_j$ and every
$n\in H$ with $n>L_j$ is a multiple of $M_j$. Its stated conclusion is
that $\sum_{n\in B}w_n/(t^n-1)$ is transcendental for every infinite
$B\subseteq H$, all bounded positive integer weights $w_n$ and every
real algebraic $t>1$.

Every divisibility chain has such cuts, and so does
$H_* = \bigcup_j N_j\{1,\ldots,2^{N_j}\}$ with $N_0=1$ and
$N_{j+1}=2N_j\operatorname{lcm}(1,\ldots,2^{N_j})$. Each block of
$H_*$ has reciprocal sum at least $1/2$ and contains antichains of
unbounded size, while the weighted sum for one prime is at most
$2t^2/(t-1)^3$ at every real $t>1$. The example thus also satisfies
the weighted criterion; we make no claim that every support satisfying
that criterion has the separation property.

The companion proof is an ordinary argument outside this paper, with no
Lean proof of transcendence, and none of the weighted, cover or mixed
theorems depends on it. Irrationality for arbitrary infinite support at
base two is the open problem.

\appendix
\section{Reciprocal-summable supports}\label{sec:reciprocal-support}
Erd\H{o}s stated the following extension of his pairwise-coprime
theorem \cite[p.~222]{erdos1968}. It follows from
Theorem~\ref{res:weighted-support}, since $h/(2^h-1)\le1$, but a direct
argument shows why reciprocal summability makes the averaging simpler.

\begin{theorem}[reciprocal-summable supports]\label{res:reciprocal-support}
Let $A\subseteq\Npos$ be infinite.  If
\[
  \sum_{a\in A}\frac1a<\infty,
\]
then \(X_A(b)\) is irrational for every integer \(b\ge2\).
\end{theorem}

Erd\H{o}s also discussed weaker conditions \cite[pp.~222, 226]{erdos1968};
we make no identification of our weighted hypothesis with the conditions
he suggested.

Reciprocal summability permits two successive limits. First the
observation length grows while the modulus stays fixed, and then the
modulus grows until each fixed exponent divides it. The two
interchanges of limit and sum use different summable bounds, and
neither requires the infinite support to have a period.

Fix the integer base $b\ge2$ and put
\[
 w_{b,d}(N)=\frac{b^{N\bmod d}}{b^d-1},\qquad
 T_N^{(b)}=\sum_{d\in A}w_{b,d}(N).
\]
Then $T_N^{(b)}-T_0^{(b)}=\Delta_{b,A}(N)$. For a fixed positive
integer $Q$, the orbit of $Q$ modulo $d$ consists of $d/g$ residues,
where $g=(Q,d)$, and summing the geometric progression gives
\begin{equation}
 M_{Q,b}(d):=\lim_{X\to\infty}\frac1X\sum_{m=1}^Xw_{b,d}(Qm)
 =\frac{g}{d(b^g-1)}\le\frac1d.
 \label{eq:gcd-orbit-mean}
\end{equation}
In the example $b=2$, $Q=4$, $d=6$ of
Section~\ref{sec:eight-return-extensions}, the mean is $1/9$ for
$w_{2,6}$ and $2/21$ for its displacement. When $d\mid Q$ we have
$w_{b,d}(Qm)=w_{b,d}(0)$, and the displacement vanishes.

To interchange the sum over exponents with this limit, we need a bound
that is summable in $d$ for the fixed modulus $Q$. Counting positive
multiples of $d$ gives
\begin{align*}
 \frac1X\sum_{m=1}^Xw_{b,d}(Qm)
 &=\sum_{r\ge1}b^{-r}
       \frac{\#\{1\le m\le X:d\mid Qm+r\}}X\\
 &\le\sum_{r\ge1}b^{-r}\frac{Q+r}{d}
 =\frac{Q/(b-1)+b/(b-1)^2}{d}\le\frac{Q+2}{d}.
\end{align*}
Indeed, the counted multiples are distinct and at most $QX+r$. For
this fixed $Q$ the majorant $(Q+2)/d$ is summable over $A$, so dominated
convergence takes the limit in the observation length inside the sum
over $d$ and gives the Ces\`aro mean $\sum_{d\in A}M_{Q,b}(d)$. The
majorant deteriorates as $Q$ grows, which is why a second limit is
needed.

Next let $Q_t=\operatorname{lcm}(1,\ldots,t)$. For each fixed $d$ we
eventually have $d\mid Q_t$ and $M_{Q_t,b}(d)=(b^d-1)^{-1}$, and a
second dominated-convergence argument, now with the bound
$M_{Q_t,b}(d)\le1/d$, yields
\begin{equation}
 \sum_{d\in A}M_{Q_t,b}(d)\longrightarrow X_A(b).
 \label{eq:lcm-prefix-orbit-limit}
\end{equation}
Hence the limiting displacement means tend to zero. We choose $t$,
then a sufficiently long finite average, and then a term no larger than
that average. The resulting arbitrarily small positive displacements
contradict the bound $1/q$ that~\eqref{eq:intro-displacement} gives
under rationality, and this proves Theorem~\ref{res:reciprocal-support}
at every integer base.

For example, the powerful integers (those whose prime exponents are all
at least two) have the form $u^2v^3$, so their reciprocal sum is at
most $\zeta(2)\zeta(3)$, and the theorem applies to every infinite set
of powerful integers. For the full set of perfect powers, compare
Duverney and Tachiya \cite[Corollary~1.2, p.~4]{duverneytachiya} and
the earlier linear-independence results of Luca and
Tachiya~\cite{lucatachiya2014independence}.

\section{Finite-support denominator periods}\label{sec:period}

For a finite support, the least common multiple of the selected
exponents survives every cancellation: it is the multiplicative order
of the base modulo the reduced denominator.

\begin{theorem}[the exact denominator period]\label{res:period}
Let $F\subseteq\Npos$ be finite and nonempty, let $b\ge2$ be an integer, and
let $D_F>0$ be the denominator of $X_F(b)$ in lowest terms.  Then $D_F$ is
coprime to $b$, and
\[
  \operatorname{ord}_{D_F}(b)=\lcm\{n:n\in F\}.
\]
If moreover $\lcm(F)\ge2$, then $\lcm(F)<D_F$.  We use
$\operatorname{ord}_1(b)=1$, so the statement includes $F=\{1\}$
at $b=2$.
\end{theorem}

Put $L=\operatorname{lcm}(F)$. Clearing denominators gives
$D_F\mid b^L-1$, so the order divides $L$. For the reverse divisibility,
fix a divisibility-maximal $n\in F$ with $n\ge2$ and choose a prime
$\ell\mid\Phi_n(b)$. With $e=v_\ell(b^n-1)$, the cyclotomic fact in
Section~\ref{app:elementary-details} gives
$\operatorname{ord}_{\ell^e}(b)=n$ for the full prime power $\ell^e$.
Every other selected exponent $m$ satisfies $n\nmid m$, hence
$v_\ell(b^m-1)<e$, so the $n$th summand has the uniquely smallest
$\ell$-adic valuation and cannot cancel. Thus $\ell^e\mid D_F$ and
$n\mid\operatorname{ord}_{D_F}(b)$. Running over all maximal selected
exponents proves the order statement, and the size bound follows from
$\operatorname{ord}_{D_F}(b)\mid\varphi(D_F)<D_F$ when $L\ge2$. For
$F=\{1\}$ the order is one directly.

The unique-valuation argument also allows signs $\pm1$ on the finite
summands; this signed extension lies outside the formal statement. The
difference between primes and prime powers is visible in
\[
 X_{\{2,3\}}(2)=\frac{10}{21},\qquad
 X_{\{2,6\}}(2)=\frac{22}{63}.
\]
In both examples $2$ has multiplicative order six modulo the
denominator. The first combines the orders two and three, while the
second retains the prime power $9$, modulo which $2$ has order six,
although no prime divisor of $63$ has order six. At base two the
boundary case $F=\{1\}$ has $D_F=L=1$. Lower bounds for denominators
give no upper control of the heights of infinite partial sums, and so
by themselves decide no value of an infinite subseries. Van Assche
instead constructs approximants for the full Lambert series and proves
both the nonvanishing and the decay of the associated integer linear
forms \cite[Lemma~1 and (34)--(35)]{vanassche2001}; that approximation
mechanism requires more than the survival of denominators.


In particular, $1/21$ is not a finite subseries sum. If it were, the
order of $2$ modulo $21$ would force every selected exponent to divide
$6$. The exponent $1$ is excluded since its weight is $1$, any sum
containing $2$ or $3$ exceeds $1/21$, and the only other nonzero sum is
$1/63$, from the exponent $6$. The next appendix uses this to separate
finite from infinite representations.

\section{Rational membership}\label{sec:rational-membership}
The preceding results start from a support and prove irrationality. We
now fix a target value and ask whether some support represents it. The
first recurrence below is a necessary condition for a rational value,
and the greedy recurrence then gives an exact criterion for membership;
neither supplies the arithmetic information still missing for $1/2$ and
$1/21$.

\subsection{Rational values and scaled tails}\label{sec:forced}
Suppose $X_A(2)=p/v$, where $p\in\Z$ and $v\ge1$ is an integer.
Multiplying the coefficient-series identity by $v2^N$ gives
\[
 z_N:=v\sum_{r\ge1}c_A(N+r)2^{-r}
     =2^Np-v\sum_{n=1}^{N}c_A(n)2^{N-n}\in\Z.
\]
Separating the first term of the tail then gives
$z_{N+1}=2z_N-vc_A(N+1)$, in which every coefficient $c_A(n)$ is a
divisor count of the same set $A$.

For nonempty $A$, any $a_0=\min A$ consecutive integers contain a
multiple of $a_0$, so the runs of zeros in $c_A$ have length at most
$a_0-1$. For infinite $A$ we choose $k$ exponents and a common multiple
$L$; then $c_A(L)\ge k$, and the scaled tail satisfies
$\sum_{r\ge1}c_A(L-1+r)2^{-r}\ge k/2$. Thus the scaled tails are
unbounded.

The compatibility condition on the coefficients is the Dirichlet
convolution identity $\mu*c_A=\mathbf1_A$, where $\mu$ is the
M\"obius function, so a putative recurrence with integral forcing must
satisfy $(\mu*c_A)(n)\in\{0,1\}$ for all $n$. The telescoping proof of
the integer-recurrence criterion and the M\"obius inversion argument
are given under
\papersectionlink{erdos257-mersenne-reasoning-surface.pdf}{record257:coefficient-recurrence}
{Bounds for a general coefficient sequence} in the companion paper,
whose example $A=\{2\}$ has value $1/3$. The correspondence admits
finite supports, so a counterexample to Problem~\ref{res:problem} must
in addition be infinite.

\subsection{The achievement set}\label{sec:geometry}
Let $w_n=(2^n-1)^{-1}$ and
\[
 \Ach=\left\{\sum_{n\ge1}\varepsilon_nw_n:
                   \varepsilon_n\in\{0,1\}\right\},\qquad
 R_N=\sum_{n>N}w_n.
\]
The estimate
\[
 2^{-N}<R_N\le2^{-N}+\frac23\,4^{-N}<w_N\qquad(N\ge1)
\]
follows on writing $w_n=2^{-n}+4^{-n}/(1-2^{-n})$, with
$n\ge N+1\ge2$ for the middle bound. If two selectors first differ at
$n$, their sums differ by at least $w_n-R_n>0$, which proves
uniqueness. Since every weight exceeds its tail, Hornich's
theorem~\cite{hornich1941}, as proved by
Nitecki~\cite[Theorem~4(1), p.~9]{nitecki2013}, shows that $\Ach$ is a
Cantor set of measure $\lim_N2^NR_N$, and the estimate above gives
$2^NR_N\to1$, so $\lambda(\Ach)=1$. Concretely, fixing the first $N$
digits gives $2^N$ disjoint closed intervals of length $R_N$; these
unions decrease to $\Ach$, and continuity of Lebesgue measure from
above gives the same limit. Kova\v{c} and Tao record this strict-tail
inequality and the Cantor conclusion in the fixed-base
setting~\cite[Remark~4.1]{kovactao}.

The
\papersectionlink{erdos257-mersenne-reasoning-surface.pdf}{record257:geometry}
{geometry section of the companion paper} also treats restricted
supports. For any allowed set $J$ of exponents, removing exponents can
only decrease the tail of each retained weight, so the same inequality
gives injectivity. If $J$ omits exactly the finite set $F$, its
achievement set has measure $2^{-|F|}$, and if $J$ omits infinitely
many exponents, the measure is zero.

\subsection{The greedy recurrence}\label{sec:actual-repairs}
For $x\ge0$ the greedy rule starts with $r_0=x$ and, at each rank
$n\ge1$, selects $n$ when $r_{n-1}\ge(2^n-1)^{-1}$, in which case it
subtracts that weight. Let $A_x$ be the resulting support and
$c_x(n)=\#\{a\in A_x:a\mid n\}$, and set
\[
 P_0=0,\qquad P_{N+1}=2P_N+c_x(N+1),\qquad
 Q_N=\lfloor2^Nx\rfloor-P_N.
\]
Here $P_N=\sum_{j=1}^N2^{N-j}c_x(j)$ truncates the coefficient
series~\eqref{eq:incidence}, whereas $r_N$ truncates the selected
Lambert weights: $Q_N$ measures the error of coefficient truncation,
and $r_N$ is the real remainder after $N$ greedy decisions. The bound
$P_N\le2^NX_{A_x}(2)\le2^Nx$ gives $Q_N\ge0$, and
\begin{equation}
 Q_{N+1}=2Q_N+\beta_N-c_x(N+1),\qquad
 \beta_N=\lfloor2^{N+1}x\rfloor-2\lfloor2^Nx\rfloor\in\{0,1\}.
 \label{eq:actual-repair-recurrence}
\end{equation}

The recurrence is integral for every real target. Writing
$\delta=x-X_{A_x}(2)\ge0$, we obtain from the coefficient expansion
\[
 Q_N=\left\lfloor 2^N\delta+
             \sum_{r\ge1}c_x(N+r)2^{-r}\right\rfloor.
\]
For a represented target $\delta=0$ and $Q_N=O(\sqrt N)$, which leaves
too little room for unit increases throughout a long window. For an
unrepresented target, $2^N\delta$ eventually dominates the divisor
count in the recurrence and forces strict increase. The theorem below
makes both halves precise.


\begin{theorem}[membership and nonincreasing integer remainders]\label{res:general-repair}
For every real $x\ge0$, the following are equivalent:
\[
 \begin{gathered}
 x\in\Ach;\\
 \forall K\ge0\ \exists N\ge K:\quad Q_{N+1}\le Q_N;\\
 \forall K\ge0\ \exists N\in[K,K+2\lfloor\sqrt K\rfloor+12):
 \quad Q_{N+1}\le Q_N.
 \end{gathered}
\]
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
For a represented target the remaining sum is at least $w_n$ exactly
when $n$ belongs to the representing support, since $R_n<w_n$. Hence
the greedy rule recovers that support, and
\[
 0\le Q_N\le\sum_{r\ge1}c_x(N+r)2^{-r}
 \le2\sqrt N+4,
\]
since $c_x(n)\le\tau(n)\le2\sqrt n$ and
$\sqrt{N+r}\le\sqrt N+\sqrt r\le\sqrt N+r$.
Put $s=\lfloor\sqrt K\rfloor$ and $T=2s+12$. Strict increase at all
$T$ steps would give $Q_{K+T}\ge T$, but $K+T<(s+4)^2$ gives
$Q_{K+T}<2s+12=T$. This proves the window condition, and hence the
cofinal nonincreases.

Conversely, if $x\notin\Ach$, then $\delta>0$ and
$Q_N\ge2^N\delta-1$, which eventually exceeds $c_x(N+1)\le N+1$.
Equation~\eqref{eq:actual-repair-recurrence} then makes $Q_N$ strictly
increasing, contradicting the cofinal nonincreases.
\end{proof}

The nonincreases must occur along the target's own greedy sequence.
For $x=0$ no exponent is selected and $Q_N=0$ at every rank, and every
finite subseries sum is likewise represented and satisfies the
criterion. The target $x=3/4$ lies strictly between $R_1<2/3$ and
$w_1=1$, so it is unrepresented and its integer remainders eventually
increase strictly. For $1/2$ and $1/21$ the theorem leaves open which
alternative occurs.

An ordinary extension of the argument, beyond the square-root window
proved in Lean, gives a uniform subpower window: for each
$0<\varepsilon<1$, a window of length
$\lceil C_\varepsilon(K+1)^\varepsilon\rceil$ suffices for a suitable
constant $C_\varepsilon$. Indeed, $\tau(n)\le A_\varepsilon n^\varepsilon$ gives
$Q_N\le D_\varepsilon(N+1)^\varepsilon$ for represented targets,
where $D_\varepsilon=A_\varepsilon\sum_{r\ge1}r^\varepsilon2^{-r}$.
Choose $C_\varepsilon>D_\varepsilon(C_\varepsilon+3)^\varepsilon$,
which is possible because $\varepsilon<1$.
For $T=\lceil C_\varepsilon(K+1)^\varepsilon\rceil$, strict increase
at all $T$ steps would give
\[
 T\le Q_{K+T}\le D_\varepsilon(K+T+1)^\varepsilon
 \le D_\varepsilon(C_\varepsilon+3)^\varepsilon(K+1)^\varepsilon<T,
\]
a contradiction. The converse again follows from exponential growth
when $x$ is unrepresented. Like the square-root window, the shorter
window leaves the membership of any particular target undecided.

At $x=1/2$ the digits $\beta_N$ vanish for $N\ge1$, and at $x=1/21$
they are six-periodic. For these two targets the missing arithmetic
input is therefore
\begin{equation}
 \forall K\ \exists N\ge K:\qquad
 c_x(N+1)\ge Q_N+\beta_N,
 \qquad x\in\{1/2,1/21\}.
 \label{eq:actual-selector-obligation}
\end{equation}
Here $Q_N$ and $c_x$ must arise from the same greedy support $A_x$.
The companion paper keeps counterexamples to fixed-multiplier
schedules, with their finite phase masks; they concern those schedules
only and decide neither target.

\section{Integer quotients, approximation and further questions}\label{sec:open}
We now give two further tests. For $1/21$ we compare integer greedy
remainders with the real greedy sequence, and for $1/2$ we allow
arbitrary finite approximating supports. Both tests require suitable
data at unbounded depths.

\paragraph{Integer quotients for $1/21$.}
For $M,d\ge1$, set
\[
 q_M(d)=\left\lfloor\frac{2^M}{2^d-1}\right\rfloor,
 \qquad T_M=\left\lfloor\frac{2^M}{21}\right\rfloor.
\]
For $d\ge2$ the geometric expansion gives
$q_M(d)=\sum_{j=1}^{\lfloor M/d\rfloor}2^{M-jd}$, because the omitted
fraction $2^{M\bmod d}/(2^d-1)$ is less than $1$; thus $q_M(d)$ is the
coefficient prefix contributed by the exponent $d$. Starting with the
remainder $T_{2R}$, we run through $d=2,\ldots,R$ in order and subtract
$q_{2R}(d)$ whenever it does not exceed the current remainder. Let
$D_R$ be the set of selected exponents and $s_R$ the final remainder,
so that
\[
 s_R=T_{2R}-\sum_{d\in D_R}q_{2R}(d)\ge0.
\]
The precision is $4^R$, although $D_R$ records decisions only through
rank $R$; the agreement condition below also uses a second run at the
same precision, continued through rank $2R$. For example, $R=6$ gives
$T_{12}=195$, $D_6=\{5\}$ and $s_6=195-\lfloor4096/31\rfloor=63\le64$.
This single row satisfies the bound in part~(2) of the theorem below,
and says nothing about whether such rows occur at unbounded depths.


Write $r_n=r_n(1/21)$ for the real greedy remainder and $A_{1/21}$ for
its support. The condition $\mathcal F_{21}$ asserts that there are
integers $n,R_0,K_0\ge0$ with all of the following properties:
\begin{enumerate}
\item $r_n>R_n$, the set of positive exponents outside $A_{1/21}$ is
finite, and every exponent greater than $n$ belongs to $A_{1/21}$;
\item for every $R\ge R_0$, the integer rule with target $T_{2R}$ and
weights $q_{2R}(2),\ldots,q_{2R}(2R)$ selects exactly the same exponents
as the real greedy rule on $\{2,\ldots,2R\}$;
\item for every $K\ge K_0$, the interval $(K,2K]$ contains an exponent
of $A_{1/21}$.
\end{enumerate}
A positive limiting remainder gives a tail violation and the eventual
selection of every exponent, hence the first and last clauses; the
middle clause asks in addition for agreement with the integer rules.

\begin{theorem}[integer-quotient tests for $1/21$]
\label{res:one-over-twenty-one-frontier}
The following statements hold.
\begin{enumerate}
\item $1/21\in\Ach$ if and only if $\mathcal F_{21}$ does not hold.
\item If there is an unbounded sequence of ranks $R$ with $s_R\le 2^R$,
  then $1/21\in\Ach$.
\item On $\mathcal F_{21}$, eventually $s_R>2^R$, the boundary rank $R+1$
  belongs to $D_{R+1}$, and, for all sufficiently large $R$,
\[
 \begin{aligned}
 D_{R+1}&=D_R\cup\{R+1\},\\
 s_{R+1}&=4s_R+
 \left\lfloor\frac{4(2^{2R}\bmod21)}{21}\right\rfloor
 -2c_{D_R}(2R+1)-c_{D_R}(2R+2)\\
 &\hspace{3em}{}-(2^{R+1}+1).
 \end{aligned}
\]
Here $c_{D_R}(m)=\#\{d\in D_R:d\mid m\}$.
\end{enumerate}
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
Write $x=1/21$, $G=A_{1/21}$ and $\delta=x-X_G(2)\ge0$. If
$x\notin\Ach$, then $\delta>0$, and since the real remainder tends to
$\delta$, every sufficiently small weight is selected and eventually
$r_n>R_n$. For the integer comparisons we first choose $d_0$ with
$w_d<\delta/2$ for $d>d_0$. The comparisons through $d_0$ are strict,
since equality would give a finite representation of $x$. After
division by $4^R$, each quotient weight differs from $w_d$ by less than
$4^{-R}$, and the remainders differ by at most $d/4^R$ whenever the
earlier decisions agree, so for large $R$ the first $d_0$ decisions
agree. At the later ranks $d\le2R$ the normalised remainder exceeds
$\delta/2>w_d$, since $2R/4^R<\delta/2$, and both rules select every
remaining rank through $2R$. This proves $\mathcal F_{21}$. A
represented target has $r_n\le R_n$ at every rank, which gives the
converse in (1).

For every $R$ the rounding errors satisfy
\begin{equation}
 \left|x-X_{D_R}(2)-\frac{s_R}{4^R}\right|
 \le\frac{R+1}{4^R}.
 \label{eq:twenty-one-rounding}
\end{equation}
Thus $s_R\le2^R$ at unbounded ranks gives finite subseries sums tending
to $x$, and since $\Ach$ is closed this proves (2), with no
compatibility required between the sets $D_R$; by
Section~\ref{sec:period} the representation obtained is infinite.

On $\mathcal F_{21}$ the eventual agreement gives
$D_R=G\cap\{2,\ldots,R\}$ and $D_{R+1}=D_R\cup\{R+1\}$, and the
rounding bound implies $s_R/4^R\to\delta>0$. Finally, for $d\in D_R$
(so $d\ge2$) we use
$q_{M+2}(d)=4q_M(d)+2\mathbf1_{d\mid M+1}+\mathbf1_{d\mid M+2}$,
together with the analogous identity for the target and
$q_{2R+2}(R+1)=2^{R+1}+1$, to obtain (3). The full comparison induction
and both quotient identities are given in
\papersectionlink{erdos257-mersenne-reasoning-surface.pdf}{record257:twenty-one-quotients}
{the companion paper's integer-quotient proof for $1/21$}.
\end{proof}


\noindent In the list $q_{2R}(2),\ldots,q_{2R}(R)$ each weight exceeds
the sum of its successors by at least $2^R+1$, so distinct subset sums
are separated by more than $2^R$, and the greedy sum is the largest
one not exceeding the target. Thus, if
$D\subseteq\{2,\ldots,R\}$ and an integer $s$ satisfy
$\sum_{d\in D}q_{2R}(d)+s=T_{2R}$ with $0\le s\le2^R$, then
$D=D_R$ and $s=s_R$
(\mword{Erdos249257/TwentyOneQuotientGreedy.lean}{231}{twentyOneClosedRow_forces_quotientGreedy}{uniqueness of a finite representation with the stated remainder bound}).
Under their alignment hypotheses, the finite crossing lemmas cited

occur, and a real greedy exponent must be skipped
(\mword{Erdos249257/TwentyOneQuotientGreedy.lean}{5179}{twentyOneAlignedSaturatedCrossing_forces_canonical_ancestor_hole}{the missing-prefix consequence of an aligned crossing},

\paragraph{Approximation to $1/2$ by finite supports.}
For $A\subseteq\{2,\ldots,M\}$ and $1\le m\le M$, define the integer
\[
 K_A(m)=2^{m-1}-\sum_{j=2}^{m}2^{m-j}c_A(j).
\]
It is the error of the truncated divisor-coefficient sum, multiplied by
$2^m$, and it satisfies $K_A(1)=1$ and $K_A(m+1)=2K_A(m)-c_A(m+1)$.

\begin{theorem}[finite approximations with vanishing scaled error]
\label{res:terminalhalf}
Suppose there are integers $M_j\ge1$ tending to infinity and sets
$A_j\subseteq\{2,\ldots,M_j\}$ such that
\[
 \frac{|K_{A_j}(M_j)|}{2^{M_j}}\longrightarrow0.
\]
Then $X_A(2)=1/2$ for some infinite set $A\subseteq\Npos$.
\end{theorem}

Only the terminal coefficient error needs control, because the omitted
tail obeys the following uniform bound, whatever the supports $A_j$ and
the earlier values of $K_{A_j}$:
\[
 \left|X_{A_j}(2)-\frac12\right|
 \le \frac{|K_{A_j}(M_j)|+2\sqrt{M_j}+4}{2^{M_j}}.
\]
Indeed, $c_{A_j}(n)\le\tau(n)\le2\sqrt n$ and
$\sqrt{M+r}\le\sqrt M+r$ bound the normalised tail by
$\sum_{r\ge1}2^{-r}(2\sqrt M+2r)=2\sqrt M+4$. Thus
$X_{A_j}(2)\to1/2$, the closedness of $\Ach$ gives a representing
support, and the odd reduced denominators of finite sums make that
support infinite. A terminal error of size $O(\sqrt{M_j})$ suffices,
although the theorem allows any error of size $o(2^{M_j})$. For a finite $D\subseteq\{2,\ldots,M\}$, the
floor-quotient identity gives
\[
 K_D(M)=2^{M-1}-\sum_{d\in D}
                \left\lfloor\frac{2^M}{2^d-1}\right\rfloor.
\]
The exact quotient rows considered in the companion paper satisfy
$\sum_{d\in D}q_M(d)=2^{M-1}-1$, so they have $K_D(M)=1$, whereas the
terminal criterion allows a larger error of either sign. At a fixed
depth these are different conditions, although the cofinal existence
of either kind of approximation characterises $1/2\in\Ach$.

Conversely, if $X_A(2)=1/2$, then $1\notin A$, and the prefixes
$A_M=A\cap\{2,\ldots,M\}$ satisfy
\[
 K_{A_M}(M)=\sum_{r\ge1}c_A(M+r)2^{-r}
       \le 2\sqrt M+4.
\]
The right-hand side uses the full support $A$, since truncation leaves
its coefficients through $M$ unchanged. Thus the existence hypothesis of
Theorem~\ref{res:terminalhalf} is equivalent to $1/2\in\Ach$, and a
proof that such approximations exist at unbounded depths would give a
negative answer to Problem~\ref{res:problem}.

\paragraph{Finite families with a shared prefix.}
Put $B(m)=2\lfloor\sqrt m\rfloor+4$.  For $0\le K\le M$, consider a
family $A_1,\ldots,A_{B(M)}\subseteq\{2,\ldots,M\}$ with
\[
 1\le K_{A_k}(m)\le B(m)\quad(1\le m\le M),
 \qquad K_{A_k}(M)=k.
\]
Require that all the $A_k$ agree on $\{1,\ldots,K\}$ and that some
integer $E\ge B(M)$ satisfies
\[
 \sum_{j=K+1}^{M}1_{A_k}(j)\,2^{M-j}+k=E
 \qquad(1\le k\le B(M)).
\]
Thus the binary suffixes take the consecutive values $E-k$, and the
hypothesis asks for a whole family at each available depth, where
Theorem~\ref{res:terminalhalf} asks for one approximant.

\begin{theorem}[unbounded shared-prefix families represent one half]
\label{res:cylinderhalf}
Suppose that for every $N$ there are $M,K$ with
$\max\{N,1\}\le M$, $0\le K\le M$, and a family satisfying all the
conditions in the preceding paragraph.  Then $X_A(2)=1/2$ for some
infinite set $A\subseteq\Npos$.
\end{theorem}

For the proof, choose at each available depth $M$ the member with
terminal carry $1$. Its scaled terminal error is $2^{-M}$, so
Theorem~\ref{res:terminalhalf} applies along unbounded depths; the
other shared-prefix conditions play no part in this compactness step.
We have no construction of such families at unbounded depths.

The tests above leave three kinds of arithmetic information to be
supplied: returns of the actual greedy sequence, finite approximants at
unbounded depths, or the exclusion of a possible final skip. The
following questions specify these missing inputs.

\begin{problem}[membership of 1/21 in the Mersenne achievement set]
\label{prob:one-over-twenty-one-membership}
For the greedy remainder of $1/21$, prove that arbitrarily large $N$
satisfy
\[
 2^{N+1}r_N(1/21)<\frac{2^{N+1}}{2^{N+1}-1},
 \quad\text{equivalently}\quad
 r_N(1/21)<\frac1{2^{N+1}-1}.
\]
Equivalently, exclude the condition $\mathcal F_{21}$ defined above.
It would suffice to rule out the eventual recurrence in
Theorem~\ref{res:one-over-twenty-one-frontier}(3) together with
$s_R>2^R$, or to prove $s_R\le2^R$ at arbitrarily large ranks; we do
not know whether either of these sufficient conditions is necessary.
\end{problem}

\begin{problem}[bounded returns of the scaled remainder]\label{prob:scaled-return}
For each of the targets $x=1/2$ and $x=1/21$, does the greedy remainder
$r_N(x)$ return to one bounded interval after scaling by $2^N$?
\[
 \exists B<\infty\ \forall K\ \exists N\ge K:
 \qquad 2^N r_N(x)\le B.
\]
\end{problem}

\begin{problem}[arithmetic tests for the greedy sequence]\label{prob:actual-invariant}
Can a finite-memory, $2$-adic or discrepancy argument prove $s_R\le2^R$
at arbitrarily large ranks, or rule out the eventual recurrence in
Theorem~\ref{res:one-over-twenty-one-frontier}(3)? Any such argument
must use the divisor counts of the actual greedy support. Can a bounded
window of data, consisting of $R\bmod6$, residues of $s_R$, divisor
counts at the endpoints and the finite set of eventual skips, force a
decrease or a contradiction? Or can one show instead that such
bounded-memory data cannot distinguish the actual sequence from
sequences that stay above $2^R$ without coming from any support?
\end{problem}

\begin{problem}[final-skip Diophantine exclusion]\label{prob:fatal-interval}
Let $E=\sum_{n\ge1}(2^n-1)^{-1}$.  If $1/21\notin\Ach$, let $M$ be the last
skipped exponent, $S_M$ its finite skipped prefix, and
\[
 a_M=\frac1{21}+\sum_{d\in S_M}\frac1{2^d-1}.
\]
Write $\operatorname{gap}_M=(2^M-1)^{-1}-R_M>0$. Can the arithmetic
restrictions on this last skipped exponent be used to prove
$|E-a_M|\ge\operatorname{gap}_M$, which would contradict
\[
 0<a_M-E<\operatorname{gap}_M?
\]
\end{problem}


\section{Elementary details used in the proofs}\label{app:elementary-details}
\paragraph{Cyclotomic prime-power fact.}
Let $b\ge2$, $n\ge2$, $\ell\mid\Phi_n(b)$ be prime and
$e=v_\ell(b^n-1)$. Then $\operatorname{ord}_{\ell^e}(b)=n$; the
statement includes the case $\ell=2$. For odd $\ell$, put
$d=\operatorname{ord}_{\ell}(b)$ and $s=v_\ell(b^d-1)$. The elementary
lifting identity $v_\ell(b^{dt}-1)=s+v_\ell(t)$ follows by factoring a
geometric sum when $\ell\nmid t$ and by a binomial expansion for a
factor $\ell$. In the factorisation $b^n-1=\prod_{r\mid n}\Phi_r(b)$,
subtracting these valuations over the proper divisors gives
\[
 v_\ell(\Phi_n(b))=
 \begin{cases}s&n=d,\\1&n=d\ell^j,\ j\ge1,\\0&\text{otherwise.}\end{cases}
\]
If $n=d$, then $d$ is already the least positive exponent with
$b^m\equiv1\pmod\ell$. If $n=d\ell^j$, the full valuation is $e=s+j$,
and the same lifting identity shows that the least exponent giving
valuation $e$ is $d\ell^j=n$. For $\ell=2$ the base $b$ is odd, and
factoring $b^{2t}-1$ gives
\[
 v_2(b^{2^j}-1)=v_2(b-1)+v_2(b+1)+j-1\qquad(j\ge1).
\]
The only possible indices $n\ge2$ are the powers $n=2^j$, with
$v_2(\Phi_2(b))=v_2(b+1)$ and $v_2(\Phi_{2^j}(b))=1$ for $j\ge2$, and
all other indices have valuation zero. The displayed identity again
makes $2^j$ the least exponent with the full valuation, which proves
the fact. Finally, $\Phi_n(b)>1$, so the prime $\ell$ exists: each
primitive $n$th root of unity $\zeta$ satisfies $|b-\zeta|>1$, and
their product is $\Phi_n(b)$.

\paragraph{Exact scalar cover cost.}
For $t\ge1$ put $z=\log_2t$. The substitution $y=2^\alpha$ turns the
infimum defining $\Psi(t)$ into the infimum of $y^z/(y-1)$ over
$1<y\le2$, whose derivative has the sign of $(z-1)y-z$. Hence
\[
 \Psi(t)=
 \begin{cases}t,&1\le t\le4,\\
 z^z/(z-1)^{z-1},&t>4.
 \end{cases}
\]
The minimum is attained at $\alpha=1$ for $t\le4$ and in the interior
for $t>4$, and the quotient diverges as $\alpha\downarrow0$. This
scalar identity gives no converse to the positive-cover criterion.

\subsection*{The cost for a finite set of divisors}
For the following finite divisor sets the lower bound is attained
asymptotically. Here $K_*(F)$ is the infimum of the cost $K$ of
Section~\ref{sec:comparison} over finite or countable covers of $F$,
their majorants and exponents, and positive weights of total one; in
particular a one-set cover has weight $1$ and carries no dyadic index
factor as in (V).
For $F(q,P)=\{qd:d\mid\prod_{p\in P}p\}$, where $q\ge2$ and no $p\in P$
divides $q$, put $S=\sum_{p\in P}1/p$.  If $S\ge1$, then
\begin{equation}
 \frac{e(S-1)}q\le K_*\bigl(F(q,P)\bigr)\le\frac{eS}q.
 \label{eq:optimal-cube-cost}
\end{equation}
For the lower bound we condition on $q\mid n$ and write $f_F(n)=2^Z$;
the Chinese remainder theorem gives $\mathbb EZ=S$. For $z\ge0$ and
$v=\alpha\log2>0$,
\[
 \frac{2^{\alpha z}}{2^\alpha-1}
 \ge\frac{e^{(z-1)v}}v\ge e(z-1)\quad(z>1);
\]
for $0\le z\le1$ the claimed lower bound is nonpositive.
Thus $\Psi(2^z)\ge e(z-1)$, and averaging and multiplying by the
density $1/q$ gives the lower bound. For the upper bound we use the
one-set cover with $z=1/S$ and $\alpha=\log_2(1+z)$, whose exact
positive expansion has cost
\[
 \frac1{qz}\prod_{p\in P}(1+z/p)\le\frac{eS}q.
\]
In particular $1-1/S\le qK_*(F(q,P))/(eS)\le1$, so that
$K_*(F(q,P))\sim eS/q$ as $S\to\infty$, uniformly over the permitted
choices of $q$ and $P$.
