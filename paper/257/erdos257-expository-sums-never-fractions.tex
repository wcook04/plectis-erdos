\section{The question: which infinite sums are irrational?}\label{sec:intro}

Every finite sum of fractions is a fraction, and an infinite sum can be
one too:
\[
 \frac12+\frac14+\frac18+\dots=1.
\]
Now consider
\[
 S=\frac13+\frac1{15}+\frac1{255}+\frac1{65535}+\dots,
\]
where the denominators are $2^2-1,2^4-1,2^8-1,2^{16}-1,\dots$ and the
exponents double at each step. We will prove that this sum is equal to
no fraction at all.

To see where such a proof might come from, multiply by $255$, the third
denominator:
\[
\begin{aligned}
 255S&=85+17+1+\frac{255}{65535}+\dots\\
     &=103+\frac1{257}+\dots.
\end{aligned}
\]
The first three terms have become the integer $103$, and a positive
remainder is left over. If $S=p/q$ with integers $p,q$ and $q>0$, then
multiplying by an integer and subtracting an integer leaves an integer
multiple of $1/q$, so a positive remainder must be at least $1/q$. Our
plan is to repeat this construction until the \emph{whole} remainder is
smaller than that fixed gap, whatever denominator $q$ is proposed.

The example belongs to a larger family, whose fractions are obtained by
taking a power of $2$, subtracting $1$, and taking the reciprocal:
\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{l|rrrrrrc}
\papertablehead
exponent $n$ & $1$ & $2$ & $3$ & $4$ & $5$ & $6$ & $\dots$\\
$2^n$ & $2$ & $4$ & $8$ & $16$ & $32$ & $64$ & $\dots$\\
denominator $2^n-1$ & $1$ & $3$ & $7$ & $15$ & $31$ & $63$ & $\dots$\\
term $1/(2^n-1)$ & $1$ & $\tfrac13$ & $\tfrac17$ & $\tfrac1{15}$ & $\tfrac1{31}$ & $\tfrac1{63}$ & $\dots$
\end{tabular}
\end{center}
Choose infinitely many of these terms, each at most once, and add them.
Choosing the terms amounts to choosing a set $A$ of positive integer
exponents. The opening example uses $A=\{2,4,8,16,\dots\}$, and choosing
all even exponents instead gives
\[
 A=\{2,4,6,8,\dots\}:\qquad
 \frac13+\frac1{15}+\frac1{63}+\frac1{255}+\dots.
\]
The exponent $0$ is excluded, since $2^0-1=0$, and we write
\[
 S_A=\sum_{n\in A}\frac1{2^n-1}
\]
for the sum of the terms whose exponents belong to $A$, taken in
increasing order of the exponents. A \emph{partial sum} stops after
finitely many selected terms, and to say that $S_A$ is \emph{irrational}
is to say that $S_A\ne p/q$ for all integers $p,q$ with $q>0$.

\begin{samepage}
For the geometric series at the start, the partial sums are
\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{l|cccc}
\papertablehead
number of terms $N$ & $1$ & $2$ & $3$ & $4$\\
$\tfrac12+\dots+\tfrac1{2^N}$ & $\tfrac12$ & $\tfrac34$ & $\tfrac78$ & $\tfrac{15}{16}$\\
gap to $1$ & $\tfrac12$ & $\tfrac14$ & $\tfrac18$ & $\tfrac1{16}$
\end{tabular}
\end{center}
\end{samepage}
In general the partial sum is $1-2^{-N}$, and its gap to $1$ tends to
zero; this is what the equation
$\tfrac12+\tfrac14+\tfrac18+\dots=1$ means.

Every series $S_A$ converges: for $n\ge1$,
\[
 0<\frac1{2^n-1}\le\frac1{2^{n-1}},\qquad
 \sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac1{2^{n-1}}=2.
\]
The inequality follows from $2^n-1\ge2^{n-1}$, so the partial sums of
$S_A$ increase and are bounded by $2$. A basic property of the real
numbers says that an increasing bounded sequence has a finite limit
(Appendix~\ref{app:sums}), and the question is whether this limit is
rational.

\section{Erd\H{o}s's question and the theorem}\label{sec:problem}

In a 1988 account, Erd\H{o}s wrote \cite[p.~105]{erdos1988} (excerpt):
\begin{quote}
``let $n_1<n_2<\dots$. Is it then true that
$\sum \frac{1}{2^{n_k}-1}$ is irrational, \ldots''
\end{quote}
Here the indices $k=1,2,\dots$ count the chosen exponents $n_k$. In
these terms, we shall prove the following theorem.

\begin{theorem}\label{thm:main}
Let $n_1<n_2<\dots$ be an infinite sequence of positive integers.
If $\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}1/n_k<\infty$, then
\[
 \sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\frac1{2^{n_k}-1}
\]
is irrational.
\end{theorem}

With $A=\{n_1,n_2,\dots\}$ this series is $S_A$, and the hypothesis
says that $\sum_{n\in A}1/n<\infty$. Erd\H{o}s's question for arbitrary
infinite $A$ remains open \cite{erdosproblems257}.

The hypothesis concerns the reciprocals of the \emph{exponents}, while
the conclusion concerns the fractions with denominators $2^n-1$. For the
doubling exponents one table shows the difference:
\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{l|cccc c}
\papertablehead
chosen exponent $n$ & $2$ & $4$ & $8$ & $16$ & $\dots$\\
term in the hypothesis, $1/n$ & $\tfrac12$ & $\tfrac14$ & $\tfrac18$ & $\tfrac1{16}$ & $\dots$\\
term in $S_A$, $1/(2^n-1)$ & $\tfrac13$ & $\tfrac1{15}$ & $\tfrac1{255}$ & $\tfrac1{65535}$ & $\dots$
\end{tabular}
\end{center}
The second row sums to $1$, so the theorem says that the third row sums
to an irrational number. Every $S_A$ converges, so the hypothesis
restricts $A$ further, for the sake of the irrationality proof.

\begin{samepage}
We shall use the hypothesis in the following form: beyond a
sufficiently large exponent, the remaining reciprocals $1/n$ add up to
less than any positive target we specify. Such a remaining sum is
called a \emph{tail}.\par
\end{samepage}

\begin{samepage}
The square exponents $A=\{1,4,9,16,\dots\}$ also satisfy the
hypothesis. Indeed, for $m\ge2$,
\[
 \frac1{m^2}<\frac1{m(m-1)}=\frac1{m-1}-\frac1m,
\]
so the comparison sum cancels:
\[
 (1-\tfrac12)+(\tfrac12-\tfrac13)+(\tfrac13-\tfrac14)
 +\dots+(\tfrac1{N-1}-\tfrac1N)=1-\tfrac1N<1.
\]
Thus $\sum_{m=2}^{N}1/m^2<1$, so $\sum_{m=1}^{\infty}1/m^2$ converges,
and the theorem proves that
$1+\tfrac1{15}+\tfrac1{511}+\tfrac1{65535}+\dots$ is irrational.\par
\end{samepage}

Choosing every positive integer, on the other hand, puts the divergent
harmonic series into the hypothesis. To see the divergence, group its
terms as $1$; $1/2$; $1/3+1/4$; $1/5+1/6+1/7+1/8$; and so on. For
example,
\[
 \frac15+\frac16+\frac17+\frac18\ge4\cdot\frac18=\frac12.
\]
Each later block doubles the number of terms and halves their smallest
value, so it too contributes at least $1/2$, and these repeated
contributions make the partial sums unbounded. The even exponents fail
the hypothesis as well, since their reciprocal sum is half the harmonic
series, and the theorem makes no assertion about either choice.
Infinitude matters too: $A=\{2,4\}$ has a finite reciprocal sum, but
$S_A=\tfrac13+\tfrac1{15}=\tfrac25$ (Question~\ref{chk:hypotheses}).

Erd\H{o}s proved the theorem in 1968 under the extra assumption that no
two chosen exponents share a divisor greater than $1$, and remarked that
this assumption could be removed, without giving the details
\cite[p.~222]{erdos1968}. We explain a proof that does without it.

The proof has three stages. First, rationality forces a fixed lower
bound on certain positive remainders. Next, the special form of the
denominators $2^n-1$ lets us calculate those remainders. Finally, an
average guarantees at least one choice whose \emph{total} remainder lies
below that lower bound. The doubling exponents show the construction at
work before any averaging is needed, and the main proof ends in
Section~\ref{sec:allchoices}. Appendices~\ref{app:sums}--\ref{app:proof}
justify the supporting facts about infinite sums and divisibility, and
can be read as needed or after the main proof; the extension to other
bases and the comparison with the formal proof are further reading.

\section{A rational number has a fixed positive gap}\label{sec:test}

Start with $2/5$. Multiplying it by an integer and subtracting an
integer always gives an integer multiple of $1/5$:
\[
 3\cdot\frac25-1=\frac15,\quad
 4\cdot\frac25-1=\frac35,\quad
 5\cdot\frac25-2=0,\quad
 1\cdot\frac25-1=-\frac35.
\]
No such value lies strictly between $0$ and $1/5$, because the
denominator fixes a smallest possible positive result.

The same reasoning applies to any fraction. From now on we write
$S=S_A$ for the series belonging to our chosen set $A$ and suppose that
$S=p/q$, where $p\in\mathbb Z$ and $q$ is a positive integer. For
integers $u,j$,
\[
 uS-j=\frac{up-jq}{q}.
\]
The numerator is an integer, so if $uS-j>0$, then $up-jq\ge1$ and
\[
 uS-j\ge\frac1q.
\]
Positivity matters here, since zero and negative values are perfectly
compatible with rationality.

To prove our sum irrational, we shall therefore suppose that $S=p/q$ and
construct integers $u,j$, which may depend on $q$, such that
\begin{equation}\label{eq:goal}
 0<uS-j<\frac1q.
\end{equation}

\begin{samepage}
\section{Multiplying the series and retaining fractional parts}\label{sec:split}

We want a multiplier that turns some terms into integers. Choose a
positive integer $k$ and consider $2^k-1$; from now on $k$ is the
exponent of the multiplier and no longer indexes the list
$n_1,n_2,\dots$. For example, $15=2^4-1$ is divisible by $3=2^2-1$.
Here is what multiplying by $15$ does to the first few terms:
\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{c|c|c|c|c}
\papertablehead
exponent $n$ & original term & multiplied term & integer part & fractional part\\
$2$ & $1/3$ & $15/3=5$ & $5$ & $0$\\
$3$ & $1/7$ & $15/7=2+1/7$ & $2$ & $1/7$\\
$4$ & $1/15$ & $15/15=1$ & $1$ & $0$\\
$5$ & $1/31$ & $15/31$ & $0$ & $15/31$
\end{tabular}
\end{center}
\end{samepage}
The rows illustrate the operation, and in the chosen series we keep
only the rows with $n\in A$. For $x\ge0$ the integer part
$\lfloor x\rfloor$ is the greatest integer at most $x$, and subtracting
it leaves the fractional part $\{x\}=x-\lfloor x\rfloor\in[0,1)$. For
example, $2\le15/7<3$, so we subtract $2$ and keep $1/7$. Subtracting
integer parts in this way keeps every contribution nonnegative.

\paragraph{The remainder can be read from the exponents.}
Keep the denominator $2^n-1$ fixed and add $n$ to $k$. Then the identity
\[
 2^{k+n}-1=2^k(2^n-1)+(2^k-1)
\]
gives
\[
 \frac{2^{k+n}-1}{2^n-1}=2^k+\frac{2^k-1}{2^n-1}.
\]
The added term $2^k$ is an integer, so the fractional part is
unchanged. Writing $k=an+r$ with $0\le r<n$ and subtracting $n$ from the
exponent repeatedly, we reduce the calculation to $r$, and since
$0\le2^r-1<2^n-1$,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:fracpart-main}
 \lft_n(k):=\left\{\frac{2^k-1}{2^n-1}\right\}
 =\frac{2^{k\bmod n}-1}{2^n-1}.
\end{equation}
Here $k\bmod n$ denotes the remainder $r$ on division by $n$, which
lies between $0$ and $n-1$, and $\lft_n(k)$ is the fractional part
calculated from it; summing these fractional parts will give $R(k)$. For
example, $8=2\cdot3+2$, so $\{255/7\}=(2^2-1)/7=3/7$. The fractional
part vanishes when $n$ divides $k$ and only then, so the multiplier
$2^k-1$ turns a selected term into an integer if and only if its
exponent divides $k$. This is why we chose this multiplier: by taking
$k$ divisible by some selected exponents, we make their contributions
vanish.

\pagebreak[0]
\begin{samepage}
\paragraph{What happens to the entire sum.}
We apply the same multiplier to every selected term, subtract each
integer part, and sum the fractional parts:
\[
 R(k)=\sum_{n\in A}\lft_n(k),\qquad
 J(k)=\sum_{\substack{n\in A\\ n\le k}}
       \left\lfloor\frac{2^k-1}{2^n-1}\right\rfloor.
\]
Only exponents $n\le k$ contribute to $J(k)$, since for $n>k$ the
multiplied term is already less than $1$; thus $J(k)$ is a finite sum of
integers. Splitting the terms gives
\begin{equation}\label{eq:split-main}
 (2^k-1)S=J(k)+R(k).
\end{equation}
The series defining $R(k)$ converges by comparison with $(2^k-1)S$, and
Appendix~\ref{app:split} justifies the identity using partial sums.\par
\end{samepage}

The sum $R(k)$ may exceed $1$, so it need not be a fractional part
itself. For the three exponents $2,3,6$ and $k=5$, the separate
fractional parts sum to
\[
 \left\{\frac{31}3\right\}+\left\{\frac{31}7\right\}
 +\left\{\frac{31}{63}\right\}
 =\frac13+\frac37+\frac{31}{63}=\frac{79}{63}>1.
\]
The fractional part of the whole product, by contrast, is $16/63$. We keep
the separate contributions because each has the formula
\eqref{eq:fracpart-main}, and their sum still has the required form
$uS-j$.

Finally, if $A$ is infinite, there is always a selected exponent
$n>k$. Its multiplied term is positive and less than $1$, so it makes a
positive contribution to $R(k)$, and all other contributions are
nonnegative. Therefore
\[
 S=p/q\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
 R(k)=(2^k-1)S-J(k)\ge\frac1q\quad\hbox{for every }k\ge1.
\]
Our task is now to find \emph{one} $k$ with $R(k)<1/q$
(Question~\ref{chk:positive}).

\section{A complete example: the doubling exponents}\label{sec:doubling}

We return to the opening example, $A=\{2,4,8,16,\dots\}$, and can now
see why $255$ cleared the first three terms: it is $2^8-1$, and the
exponents $2,4,8$ all divide $8$. In the new notation the calculation
reads
\[
 255S=103+R(8),\qquad R(8)=\frac1{257}+\frac1{16843009}+\dots>0.
\]
One small remainder rules out only some denominators, and to rule out
every $q$ we need remainders as small as we please. For any doubling
exponent $k$, all selected $n\le k$ divide $k$ and contribute zero, while
every later selected exponent $n$ is at least $2k$ and gives
\[
 0<\lft_n(k)=\frac{2^k-1}{2^n-1}
 <\frac{2^k}{2^{n-1}}=\frac2{2^{n-k}}.
\]
For $k=8$ the gaps $n-k$ are $8,24,56,\dots$, and in general they are
distinct integers at least $k$. Inserting the missing positive terms, so
that the gaps run through \emph{all} integers $k,k+1,k+2,\dots$, can only
increase the upper bound, and so
\[
 0<R(k)<\sum_{e=k}^{\infty}\frac2{2^e}=\frac4{2^k}.
\]
The last equality is the formula for a geometric series whose first
term is $2/2^k$ and whose ratio is $1/2$.

\begin{samepage}
For $k=8$ this gives $R(8)<1/64$, which rules out every denominator
$q\le64$, since such a denominator would force $R(8)\ge1/q\ge1/64$; for
$k=16$ the bound is $R(16)<1/16384$. Given any proposed denominator $q$,
we choose a doubling exponent $k$ with $2^k>4q$, and then $0<R(k)<1/q$
contradicts rationality. This completes the proof for the doubling
exponents.\par
\end{samepage}

What made this work is that each $k$ both eliminated all earlier terms
and left the next exponent at least $2k$. A general set $A$ need not
have that spacing, and the next section explains how averaging takes
its place.

\begin{samepage}
\section{Why averaging works}\label{sec:general}

\paragraph{Larger multipliers need not help.}
For the square exponents $A=\{1,4,9,16,\dots\}$, both $k=4$ and $k=8$
are divisible by the first two selected exponents $1$ and $4$, and all
later selected exponents exceed $8$, so their integer parts vanish for
both multipliers. Consequently
\[
 R(4)=15\left(\frac1{511}+\frac1{65535}+\dots\right),\qquad
 R(8)=255\left(\frac1{511}+\frac1{65535}+\dots\right)=17R(4).
\]
In fact $R(4)\approx0.0296$ and $R(8)\approx0.503$: increasing $k$
while keeping the same zero contributions has enlarged the remaining
terms. Eliminating longer initial groups need not help either. In
Exercise~\ref{ex:6} the next selected exponent is $k+1$, which leaves
$(2^k-1)/(2^{k+1}-1)$, close to $1/2$. What matters is which values of
$k$ we use, and the number of initial rows they make zero is only part
of the story.\par
\end{samepage}

\subsection{One table, many attempts}

Keep $A$, and hence the original number $S$, fixed. For each new
choice of $k$ we repeat the whole calculation: multiply every selected
term by $2^k-1$, subtract its integer part, and add what remains to
obtain $R(k)$. \emph{Each column below is one complete attempt at the
contradiction}, and each row follows the contribution of one original
term across the attempts. We need one column whose total is less than
$1/q$.

We choose candidate exponents spaced $Q$ apart, $k=Q,2Q,\dots,MQ$, so
that $Q$ is the spacing, $M$ is the number of attempts, $m$ labels the
attempt $k=mQ$, and the multiplier itself is $2^{mQ}-1$. Neither $Q$
nor $k$ has to belong to $A$. We take $Q$ divisible by an initial group
of selected exponents, so that those rows stay zero in every column.
For the square exponents, $Q=4$ gives the following table.
\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{c|cccc}
\papertablehead
selected exponent $n$ & $k=4$ & $k=8$ & $k=12$ & $k=16$\\
$1$ & $0$ & $0$ & $0$ & $0$\\
$4$ & $0$ & $0$ & $0$ & $0$\\
$9$ & $15/511$ & $255/511$ & $7/511$ & $127/511$\\
$16$ & $15/65535$ & $255/65535$ & $4095/65535$ & $0$\\
$\vdots$ & $\vdots$ & $\vdots$ & $\vdots$ & $\vdots$\\\hline
column total & $R(4)$ & $R(8)$ & $R(12)$ & $R(16)$
\end{tabular}
\end{center}
Every entry is a fractional part $\lft_n(k)$; for example, the cell
$n=9,k=12$ uses $12=1\cdot9+3$, so
$\lft_9(12)=(2^3-1)/(2^9-1)=7/511$. Each column uses the same
multiplier throughout, including in the rows not displayed
(Question~\ref{chk:columns}).

\begin{samepage}
\paragraph{Average complete attempts.}
Different rows may be small in different columns, and choosing a
separate column for each row would give no single multiplier for the
original series. Instead we average \emph{every row across the same
columns} and then add the row averages. The result is the average of
the complete column totals, since both calculations add the same
entries and divide by the same number $M$. In symbols,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:average-main}
 \frac1M\sum_{m=1}^{M}R(mQ)
 =\sum_{n\in A}\frac1M\sum_{m=1}^{M}\lft_n(mQ).
\end{equation}
\end{samepage}
With infinitely many rows we first use finite portions and then let
them grow, and convergence preserves the equality
(Appendix~\ref{app:proof}). If this average is below $1/q$, then at
least one whole column has total below $1/q$, for otherwise every column
total would be at least $1/q$ and so would their average.

\paragraph{What the two estimates will do.}
We shall divide the rows into three groups. An initial finite group
stays zero. A further finite group can complete its cycles if $M$ is
chosen suitably, and we shall bound the average of each such row by
$1/n$. For the infinitely many remaining rows we need a bound valid for
every $M$, and we shall prove the bound $2Q/n$. Once $Q$ is fixed, the
sum of these bounds becomes as small as we like if we move far enough
down the rows, because the remaining reciprocals $1/n$ have arbitrarily
small total. Section~\ref{sec:proof} chooses the two row cutoffs, and
then $M$, so that both nonzero groups have small total averages.

\subsection{Complete cycles give small averages}\label{sec:cycles}

To find a rule for every row we look at $n=3$ and $n=6$, even though
they are not exponents of the square series. Start with $n=3$ and
consecutive $k$:
\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{l|ccc|ccc}
\papertablehead
$k$ & $1$ & $2$ & $3$ & $4$ & $5$ & $6$\\
$k\bmod3$ & $1$ & $2$ & $0$ & $1$ & $2$ & $0$\\
$\lft_3(k)$ & $1/7$ & $3/7$ & $0$ & $1/7$ & $3/7$ & $0$
\end{tabular}
\end{center}
One repeating block, or \emph{cycle}, has sum $4/7<1$ and average
$4/21<1/3$. The bound concerns the average only, since one entry,
$3/7$, exceeds $1/3$. For general $n$, a consecutive cycle has the
remainders $0,1,\dots,n-1$. The powers $2^0,2^1,\dots,2^{n-1}$ add up to
$2^n-1$, which is the denominator, and each numerator is one less than
the corresponding power, so the total over the cycle is
\[
 \frac{(2^0-1)+(2^1-1)+\dots+(2^{n-1}-1)}{2^n-1}
 <\frac{2^0+2^1+\dots+2^{n-1}}{2^n-1}=1.
\]
Dividing by its $n$ entries gives a cycle average below $1/n$. These
reciprocals bound the row averages, and that is why the hypothesis
$\sum_{n\in A}1/n<\infty$ controls their sum.

\pagebreak[0]
\begin{samepage}
\paragraph{Keep the required spacing.}
Our candidates move in steps of $Q$, because the spacing $Q$ is what
preserves the initial zero rows. With $n=6$ and $Q=4$ we find
\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{l|ccc}
\papertablehead
sample number $m$ & $1$ & $2$ & $3$\\
$k=4m$ & $4$ & $8$ & $12$\\
$k\bmod6$ & $4$ & $2$ & $0$\\
$\lft_6(k)$ & $15/63$ & $3/63$ & $0$
\end{tabular}
\end{center}
The last two rows repeat every three samples, only the even remainders
occur, and the average is $(15+3+0)/(3\cdot63)=2/21<1/6$.\par
\end{samepage}

\begin{samepage}
Let $g=\gcd(n,Q)$ be the \emph{greatest common divisor}, the largest
positive integer dividing both $n$ and $Q$, and put $c=n/g$.
In the example $n=6,Q=4$ these are $g=2$ and $c=3$. We shall show that
one cycle has $c$ samples, which visit $0,g,2g,\dots,n-g$ once each in
some order; knowing which remainders occur will let us compare the
sampled mean with the mean over all remainders. The argument has four
steps.

\emph{Possible remainders.} Each remainder is $mQ$ minus a multiple of
$n$, and both are divisible by $g$, so each remainder is a multiple of
$g$. Between $0$ and $n-1$ there are $c$ such multiples,
$0,g,2g,\dots,n-g$.

\emph{A return after $c$ steps.} Since
\[
 cQ=\frac ng Q=n\frac Qg,
\]
taking another $c$ steps adds a multiple of $n$ and leaves the
remainder unchanged. So $c$ is a period, and it remains to exclude an
earlier repetition.

\emph{No earlier repetition.} Two integers have the same remainder on
division by $n$ if and only if their difference is a multiple of $n$.
For samples $m_1<m_2$ that difference is $m_2Q-m_1Q=(m_2-m_1)Q$, so,
after dividing out $g$, equal remainders mean that $n/g$ divides
$(m_2-m_1)(Q/g)$. The integers $n/g$ and $Q/g$ have no common divisor
greater than $1$, that is, they are \emph{coprime}, and this lets us
cancel $Q/g$: the number $c=n/g$ must divide $m_2-m_1$. In the example
$n=6,Q=4$, write $d=m_2-m_1$; if $3$ divides $2d$, it also divides
$d=2(2d)-3d$, since both terms on the right are multiples of $3$.
Appendix~\ref{app:cycle} proves the general cancellation fact. Hence
samples fewer than $c$ steps apart have different remainders.

\emph{All possibilities are visited.} The first $c$ remainders are
distinct and there are only $c$ possible values, so every possible
remainder occurs once in each cycle.\par
\end{samepage}

\paragraph{Compare means without calculating a new formula.}
Sorting the sampled remainders $4,2,0$ as $0,2,4$ leaves the sampled
mean unchanged, and these remainders begin the pairs $(0,1)$, $(2,3)$,
$(4,5)$. Since \mbox{$(2^r-1)/(2^n-1)$} increases with $r$, the first
remainder of each pair gives its smaller fractional part. We copy each
sampled value twice and compare the copies with all six values of the
consecutive cycle (Figure~\ref{fig:cycle-mean}).

\begin{figure}[htbp]
\centering
\input{257/figures/cycle-mean.tex}
\caption{For $n=6,Q=4$, two copies of each sample double
both sum and count, preserving the mean. Each filled value is at most its
outlined counterpart, so the sampled mean is at most the consecutive-cycle
mean, which is below $1/6$.}
\label{fig:cycle-mean}
\end{figure}
\begin{samepage}
In general each group consists of $g$ consecutive remainders, and the
sample selects the first of them, giving $0,g,\dots,n-g$. Repeating each
selected value $g$ times gives a sum no larger than the sum over all
$n$ remainders. After division by $n$, the copied list has the mean of
the $c$ samples, while the mean over the consecutive cycle is below
$1/n$. Thus, whenever $M$ is a positive multiple of $c$,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:cycle-main}
 \frac1M\sum_{m=1}^{M}\lft_n(mQ)<\frac1n.
\end{equation}
The bound $1/n$ is independent of $Q$, so making the initial rows zero
may require a large spacing without worsening it, provided $M$ completes
the cycles. If $n$ divides $Q$, the cycle has the single entry $0$,
which also satisfies the bound.\par
\end{samepage}

\pagebreak[0]
\begin{samepage}
\subsection{A bound that also covers unfinished cycles}\label{sec:unfinished}

An unfinished cycle can miss its final zero. For $n=5$ and consecutive
candidates ($Q=1$), one cycle is
\[
 \frac1{31},\quad\frac3{31},\quad\frac7{31},\quad\frac{15}{31},\quad0.
\]
The first four entries average $26/(4\cdot31)=13/62>1/5$, whereas all
five average $26/(5\cdot31)=26/155<1/5$, so the increasing positive
list and the full cycle obey different bounds
(Question~\ref{chk:partial} gives a spaced example).\par
\end{samepage}

Moreover, finitely many columns cannot complete infinitely many rows:
if $n>MQ$, the last sample has not even reached the first positive
multiple of $n$. We therefore need a second estimate, valid for
\emph{every} positive $M$:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:uniform-main}
 \frac1M\sum_{m=1}^{M}\lft_n(mQ)\le\frac{2Q}{n}.
\end{equation}
For fixed $Q$ this bound holds for every $M$, so choosing the number
of columns later cannot invalidate it. We prove it with the same
cycles.

\paragraph{First bound all the consecutive entries.}
The $M$ sampled exponents $Q,2Q,\dots,MQ$ lie among the first $MQ$
positive integers, and we first bound the sum over all $MQ$ entries.
For $n\ge2$, consecutive entries beginning at $k=1$ repeat the block
\[
 \underbrace{\frac1{2^n-1},\frac3{2^n-1},\dots,
 \frac{2^{n-1}-1}{2^n-1}}_{n-1\text{ positive entries, increasing}},\quad 0.
\]
\begin{samepage}
For $n=2$ the positive list consists of $1/3$ alone. As the
calculation for a complete cycle showed, the positive entries sum to
less than $1$, so their average is less than $1/(n-1)$. No beginning
portion of this \emph{positive} list has a larger average, because each
omitted entry is at least as large as every included entry, and
appending the omitted entries therefore cannot lower the average. Thus
a nonempty beginning portion has average at most $2/n$, since
$1/(n-1)\le2/n$ for $n\ge2$. We left out the final zero for this
comparison; the whole block, zero included, already has the stronger
bound $1/n$.\par
\end{samepage}

The first $MQ$ entries consist of complete blocks followed, possibly,
by a beginning portion of the positive list; for $n=5$, for example, the
first seven entries have numerators $(1,3,7,15,0)$ followed by $(1,3)$,
all over $31$. Each piece has sum at most $2/n$ times its number of
entries, and the pieces contain $MQ$ entries altogether, so their total
is at most $2MQ/n$. For $n=1$ all entries are zero and the bound still
holds.

\paragraph{Keep only the sampled entries.}
Removing nonnegative entries cannot increase the sum, and hence
\[
 \sum_{m=1}^{M}\lft_n(mQ)
 \le\sum_{k=1}^{MQ}\lft_n(k)\le\frac{2MQ}{n}.
\]
Dividing by the number $M$ of candidates, we obtain
\eqref{eq:uniform-main}. This also explains the factor $Q$: we bounded
$M$ sampled entries by all $MQ$ consecutive entries. The candidates
themselves are unchanged, and at no point did we assume that their
sampled values increase.

\section{Combining the estimates}\label{sec:proof}

Suppose that $S=p/q$. Then every column total $R(k)$ is at least
$1/q$, and we shall contradict this by making a finite average of
column totals less than $1/q$.

\subsection{The construction with actual numbers}\label{app:numbers}

We use the doubling exponents once more, although we already have a
shorter proof for them, because they let us watch the general method at
work with actual numbers. Suppose the proposed denominator is $q=5$. It
is enough to make the average of the column totals less than $1/5$, and
we allow each of the two nonzero groups less than $1/10$. Beyond a
doubling exponent $2^h$, the reciprocal tail is
$1/2^{h+1}+1/2^{h+2}+\dots=1/2^h$.
\begin{enumerate}
\item \emph{Decide which early rows to make zero.}
 Put the first cutoff at the exponent $t=16$. The reciprocal sum of all
 later rows is $1/16<1/10$, so any middle group fits within its
 allowance, whereas the earlier cutoff $8$ would give $1/8>1/10$.
\item \emph{Preserve those zeros in every attempt.}
 Choose $Q=16$, which is divisible by $2,4,8,16$, so that every
 candidate $k=16m$ gives zero in these four rows.
\item \emph{Make the infinite far group small despite the factor $2Q$.}
 Applying $2Q/n$ to every row beyond $t=16$ would bound the sum of
 their averages only by $32/16=2$, and raising a doubling cutoff $t$
 together with $Q=t$ still gives $2Q/t=2$. This reflects a limitation of
 the estimate and says nothing about the size of $R(k)$. Instead we keep
 $Q=16$ and put the second cutoff at $T=512$, so that the remaining rows
 have exponents $1024,2048,\dots$, and adding the bounds $32/n$ on their
 averages gives
 \[
  32\left(\frac1{1024}+\frac1{2048}+\dots\right)
  =\frac{32}{512}<\frac1{10}.
 \]
 A cutoff of $256$ would leave $32/256=1/8$, more than the allowance
 $1/10$.
\item \emph{Complete all cycles between the cutoffs.}
 The middle exponents $32,64,128,256,512$ have cycle lengths
 $2,4,8,16,32$, and we choose $M=64$, a common multiple of these
 lengths. Every middle row then completes its cycles, and the far bound
 remains valid because it holds for every $M$.
\end{enumerate}
The cutoffs $t$ and $T$ are \emph{values of exponents}, and they
should be distinguished from counts of terms. The last candidate is
$MQ=1024$ while the second cutoff is $T=512$, because the number of
columns and the division of the rows into groups do different jobs. The
choice $M=32$ would also work; taking $64$ shows that we may use more
columns without changing either cutoff.

The initial rows contribute zero, and the middle row averages together
are less than
\[
 \frac1{32}+\frac1{64}+\frac1{128}+\frac1{256}+\frac1{512}
 =\frac{31}{512}.
\]
The far row averages add up to at most $2Q/T=32/512$, and hence
\[
 \frac1{64}\sum_{m=1}^{64}R(16m)
 <\frac{31}{512}+\frac{32}{512}=\frac{63}{512}<\frac15.
\]
At least one of the 64 complete attempts has $0<R(k)<1/5$, which
contradicts $S=p/5$. The argument proves that some candidate works
without identifying which, and that existence is all the contradiction
needs.

The first cutoff determines which rows $Q$ must keep zero, the second is
chosen after $Q$ is fixed to compensate for the factor $2Q$, and only
then do we choose how many columns to use (Question~\ref{chk:order}
asks what happens when $Q$ changes).

\begin{samepage}
\subsection{The same choices for any admissible set}\label{sec:allchoices}

Let $A$ satisfy the hypotheses of the theorem, and suppose that
$S_A=p/q$, where $q$ is a positive integer. Only finitely many positive
integers lie below any fixed bound, so the infinite set $A$ has
arbitrarily large elements, and moving a cutoff farther out can only
decrease a reciprocal tail. We may therefore take the cutoffs to be
elements $t<T$ of $A$, so that the initial group contains $t$ and the
middle group contains $T$. The choices are made in the following
order:
\[
 t\ \longrightarrow\ Q\ \longrightarrow\ T\ \longrightarrow\ M.
\]
\begin{enumerate}
\item Choose $t\in A$ such that $\sum_{n\in A,\,n>t}1/n<1/(2q)$,
 which is possible because the reciprocal tails tend to zero.
\item Choose a positive common multiple $Q$ of the selected exponents
 $n\le t$, for instance their product. These exponents divide every
 candidate $mQ$, so their rows are zero.
\item With $Q$ fixed, choose $T\in A$ with $T>t$ such that
 $2Q\sum_{n\in A,\,n>T}1/n<1/(2q)$; the tails tend to zero, and the
 coefficient $2Q$ is now fixed.
\item For the finitely many selected $t<n\le T$, choose $M$ to be a
 positive common multiple of their cycle lengths $n/\gcd(n,Q)$, for
 instance their product. The far-tail bound remains valid for this
 final choice of $M$.
\end{enumerate}
\end{samepage}
We add the row averages over these $M$ columns:
\begin{center}
\small
\begin{tabular}{l|p{4cm}|p{5.4cm}}
\papertablehead
selected exponents & reason for the bound & total contribution\\
$n\le t$ & each $n$ divides $Q$ & $0$\\
$t<n\le T$ & complete cycles; row average below $1/n$
 & below $\sum_{n\in A,\,n>t}1/n<1/(2q)$\\
$n>T$ & bound $2Q/n$ for every $M$
 & at most $2Q\sum_{n\in A,\,n>T}1/n<1/(2q)$
\end{tabular}
\end{center}
By \eqref{eq:average-main}, the average of the column totals is therefore
\[
 \frac1M\sum_{m=1}^{M}R(mQ)<0+\frac1{2q}+\frac1{2q}=\frac1q.
\]
At least one total is therefore below $1/q$, which contradicts the
lower bound that rationality gives for every column. This proves
Theorem~\ref{thm:main}.

Looking back, infinitude supplied the positivity of the remainders,
rationality supplied the fixed gap, and the convergence of
$\sum_{n\in A}1/n$ supplied both small tails. The order of the choices
ensured that all the bounds apply to the same finite collection of
complete attempts.

\begin{samepage}
\section{Consequences and limits}\label{sec:limits}

For every integer $b\ge2$, the same method proves
\[
 A\text{ infinite},\quad\sum_{n\in A}\frac1n<\infty
 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad
 \sum_{n\in A}\frac1{b^n-1}\text{ is irrational}.
\]
We replace the multiplier by $b^k-1$; the fractional parts become
$(b^{k\bmod n}-1)/(b^n-1)$, their cycle averages are still below $1/n$,
and Appendix~\ref{app:bases} checks both estimates.
\end{samepage}

The theorem covers every selection from the square-exponent series
$1+\tfrac1{15}+\tfrac1{511}+\dots$. If infinitely many terms remain,
their sum is irrational, because deleting terms cannot increase the
reciprocal sum $\sum_{m\ge1}1/m^2<\infty$ of the exponents, so
Theorem~\ref{thm:main} still applies; if only finitely many remain,
their sum is rational. Thus a selection from this series has a rational
sum if and only if it is finite, whatever pattern the retained exponents
follow, and the same holds with $2$ replaced by any integer $b\ge2$.

The reciprocal-sum condition is sufficient for irrationality, and the
method stops short of necessity: the remainders $R(k)$ need not become
arbitrarily small for every infinite $A$. When every positive exponent
is selected, the terms with $n>k$ alone give
\[
 R(k)\ge\sum_{n>k}\frac{2^k-1}{2^n-1}
 >\sum_{n>k}\frac{2^k-1}{2^n}
 =1-2^{-k}\ge\frac12.
\]
These remainders therefore never fall below $1/2$. Nevertheless,
Erd\H{o}s proved in
1948 that the full sum is irrational \cite{erdos1948}, and the failure
of our method says nothing about rationality. Tao and Ter\"av\"ainen
proved irrationality for the prime exponents
\cite[Theorem~1.3]{taoteravainen2025}, another case with divergent
reciprocal sum. The short paper \cite{shortpaper} gives further
sufficient conditions, and the long record \cite{longrecord} develops
their proofs and the obstructions to extending them. Erd\H{o}s's
question for every infinite set $A$ remains open.

Appendix~\ref{app:lean} describes the formal proof of the theorem and
how it differs from the finite-average proof given here.

\appendix

\section{Supporting details and formal-proof correspondence}\label{app:full}

This appendix supplies the details on convergence and limits, the
divisibility argument for sampled cycles, the estimates at other bases,
and an optional comparison with the formal proof.

\subsection{Convergence and tails}\label{app:sums}

For nonnegative terms $a_n$, the partial sums
$\sum_{n=1}^{N}a_n$ increase. If they are bounded above, let $L$ be their
least upper bound, the smallest real number that is at least every
partial sum. For every $\varepsilon>0$ some partial sum exceeds
$L-\varepsilon$, for otherwise $L-\varepsilon$ would be a smaller upper
bound, and every later partial sum then lies in $(L-\varepsilon,L]$, so
the series converges to $L$. Its tail after $N$ equals
$L-\sum_{n=1}^{N}a_n$ and tends to zero, which is the property used
twice when choosing the cutoffs in Section~\ref{sec:proof}.

Comparison of series follows by comparing partial sums: if
$0\le a_n\le b_n$ and $\sum b_n<\infty$, then
$\sum a_n\le\sum b_n<\infty$, and if one term is strictly smaller, the
sums differ by at least that positive difference. In particular,
restricting a nonnegative series to a subset of its indices can only
decrease its sum.

For $0<x<1$, multiplying out the finite geometric sum gives
\[
 (1-x)(1+x+\dots+x^{N-1})=1-x^N.
\]
Letting $N\to\infty$ yields $\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}x^j=1/(1-x)$. At
$x=1/2$ this gives $\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}2^{-j}=1$ and, by scaling,
$\sum_{j=h}^{\infty}2^{-j}=2^{1-h}$. Consequently the bound
$1/(2^n-1)\le2^{1-n}$ proves that $S_A$ converges for every set $A$,
whether or not $\sum_{n\in A}1/n$ converges.

\subsection{Passing to the full transformed sum}\label{app:split}

Fix $k$. Each $\lft_n(k)$ is nonnegative and at most
$(2^k-1)/(2^n-1)$, so $R(k)$ converges by comparison with $(2^k-1)S$,
and only selected exponents $n\le k$ have nonzero integer parts. For
any integer $L\ge k$, splitting the finitely many terms up to $L$ gives
\[
 (2^k-1)\sum_{\substack{n\in A\\n\le L}}\frac1{2^n-1}
 =J(k)+\sum_{\substack{n\in A\\n\le L}}\lft_n(k).
\]
As $L$ grows, $J(k)$ stays fixed and the two partial sums tend to $S$
and $R(k)$, which proves \eqref{eq:split-main}.

\subsection{Why the sampled remainders form a cycle}\label{app:cycle}

The elementary cancellation fact states that if positive integers
$c,u$ are coprime and $c$ divides $du$, then $c$ divides $d$. The
example in Section~\ref{sec:cycles} used $c=3$ and $u=2$, and for
general coprime $c,u$ the same argument uses integers $a,b$ with
$au+bc=1$. To see that they exist, let $v$ be the smallest positive
value of $au+bc$ as $a,b$ range over the integers; there is at least
one positive value, since $u=1\cdot u+0\cdot c$. Write $v=au+bc$ with
coefficients attaining this minimum. Division gives $u=sv+r$ with
$0\le r<v$, so
\[
 r=u-sv=(1-sa)u-sbc.
\]
Thus $r$ is another integer combination of $u$ and $c$. If it were positive, it
would contradict the minimality of $v$, so $r=0$ and $v$ divides $u$.
The same reasoning shows that $v$ divides $c$, and since $u,c$ are
coprime, $v=1$. Now $d=a(du)+b(dc)$ proves the cancellation.

Return to $g=\gcd(n,Q)$, $c=n/g$ and $u=Q/g$. The integers $c,u$ are
coprime, since a larger common divisor would give a common divisor of
$n$ and $Q$ larger than $g$. Writing $x\mid y$ to mean that $x$ divides
$y$, we see that two sampled exponents $m_1Q$ and $m_2Q$ have equal
remainders if and only if
\[
 n\mid(m_2-m_1)Q
 \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad
 c\mid(m_2-m_1)u
 \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad
 c\mid m_2-m_1.
\]
Thus the first $c$ sampled remainders are distinct and repeat after
$c$ steps. Every remainder is divisible by $g$, since both $mQ$ and $n$
are, and there are only the $c$ possibilities $0,g,\dots,n-g$, so each
occurs once. This is the remainder rule used in
Section~\ref{sec:cycles}.

\subsection{Adding the row averages}\label{app:proof}

For finitely many rows $n\le H$, adding the row averages is the same
as averaging the column totals, since both operations add the same
entries and divide by $M$. Keep $Q$ and $M$ fixed. Given any positive
target $\varepsilon$, each of the $M$ convergent column sums $R(mQ)$
has tail less than $\varepsilon$ beyond some row cutoff, and beyond the
largest of these finitely many cutoffs all column tails, and therefore
their average, are less than $\varepsilon$. Consequently the finite
sums of row averages tend to the average of the full column totals.
This proves \eqref{eq:average-main} with only finite rearrangements.

\subsection{Other bases}\label{app:bases}

Let $b\ge2$ be an integer; for $b=3$, the exponents $2,4,8,\dots$ give
$1/8+1/80+1/6560+\dots$. Convergence and the fractional-part formula
carry over, because $b^n-1\ge2^{n-1}$ and
$b^{k+n}-1=b^k(b^n-1)+(b^k-1)$.

For a consecutive cycle, the sum of the fractional parts is
\[
 \frac{(b^0-1)+\dots+(b^{n-1}-1)}{b^n-1}
 <\frac{b^0+b^1+\dots+b^{n-1}}{b^n-1}
 =\frac1{b-1}\le1.
\]
Here we used $1+b+\dots+b^{n-1}=(b^n-1)/(b-1)$ and $b\ge2$. The
fractional parts still increase with the remainder, so the comparison by
equal groups bounds the average over a complete sampled cycle by the
average over a consecutive cycle, which is below $1/n$.

For $n\ge2$ the $n-1$ positive entries still increase and have total
less than $1$, so the argument for beginning portions in
Section~\ref{sec:unfinished} gives the same bound $2/n$ for consecutive
entries starting at $k=1$, and keeping only the sampled entries gives
the bound $2Q/n$ for every sampled length $M$. For $n=1$ the fractional
parts are all zero, because $(b^k-1)/(b-1)$ is an integer. Both
estimates needed for the three groups therefore remain valid, and with
the same choices of $t,Q,T,M$ the proof shows that
$\sum_{n\in A}1/(b^n-1)$ is irrational whenever $A$ is infinite and
$\sum_{n\in A}1/n<\infty$ \cite[Theorem~A.1]{shortpaper}.

\subsection{The formal proof and how it differs}\label{app:lean}

The theorem is proved in Lean as
\href{https://github.com/wcook04/plectis-erdos/blob/436f55ebdafa67e4af0fff79f621c13f2ded12bf/lean/Erdos249257/AllBaseReciprocalSupportIrrationality.lean#L395}{\texttt{irrational\_erdosSupportSeries\_of\_summable\_reciprocal}}.
For every natural number $b\ge2$ and every infinite set $A$ of natural
numbers whose sequence of terms $1/n$ for $n\in A$ (and $0$ otherwise)
is summable, it states that the sum of $1/(b^n-1)$ over $A$ is
irrational. Lean's natural numbers start at $0$, and in Lean division by
$0$ gives $0$, so the exponent $0$ contributes $0$ to both sums; removing
it changes neither sum and leaves an infinite set infinite. At $b=2$ the
statement is therefore Theorem~\ref{thm:main}, and in general it is the
formal version of \cite[Theorem~A.1]{shortpaper}. The Lean kernel
accepts the proof, and a
\href{https://github.com/wcook04/plectis-erdos/blob/846d57d3f9926696332d782eb232aaf3cf803a99/evidence/erdos-257-mersenne-support-subseries.md#res-reciprocal-support-comparator}{Comparator check}
confirms, with a second independent kernel checker, that the proof
establishes a separately written formal statement. The finite
reconstruction in Sections~\ref{sec:test}--\ref{sec:proof} and its
estimates are ordinary proofs written for this exposition; the formal
proof follows its own route and does not check them line by line.

At base $2$ the Lean proof follows \cite[Appendix~A]{shortpaper} and
takes limits of averages; it reaches the other bases by comparing each
base-$b$ fractional part with twice the base-$2$ one, a different route
from Appendix~\ref{app:bases}. With the exponent $0$ omitted, the
shifted quantity used in Lean is
\[
 \sum_{n\in A}\frac{2^{k\bmod n}}{2^n-1}=S_A+R(k),
\]
whose value at $k=0$ is $S_A$, so a value strictly between $S_A$ and
$S_A+\varepsilon$ means $0<R(k)<\varepsilon$, the target of our proof.
In terms of our fractional parts, the formal proof first fixes $Q$ and
lets the averaging length tend to infinity. Each row average then tends
to its cycle mean, since the unfinished last cycle has bounded length,
and throughout this limit the row averages are bounded by $2(Q+1)/n$.
Because $Q$ is fixed and $\sum_{n\in A}1/n$ converges, these bounds
have small tails, which justifies passing the limit through the sum.
The proof then takes $Q=\operatorname{lcm}(1,\dots,t)$: every limiting
row average with $n\le t$ is zero, every other one is at most $1/n$,
independently of $t$, and their total is at most
$\sum_{n\in A,\,n>t}1/n$, which tends to zero. To obtain one witness it
chooses a suitable $Q$ first and a sufficiently long average second. Our
cutoffs $t,T$ and number $M$ of columns make those choices explicit,
using the separately proved estimate $2Q/n$, and under the rationality
assumption both arguments make the integer $qR(k)$ lie strictly between
$0$ and $1$.

Let $a_1=2$ and let each later exponent be one more than the product of the
earlier ones, giving $2,3,7,43,1807,\dots$. Set $A=\{a_1,a_2,\dots\}$
and write $P_j=a_1a_2\cdots a_j$.
(a) Show that $\tfrac1{a_1}+\dots+\tfrac1{a_j}=1-\tfrac1{P_j}$. (b) Show that
with $k=P_j$ the first $j$ exponents leave $0$ but the exponent $P_j+1$ leaves at
least $\tfrac37$, so $R(P_j)\ge\tfrac37$ for every $j$.
\end{exercise}

\section{Answers}\label{app:answers}
\begingroup
\setlength{\parskip}{0.7ex plus 0.2ex}
\setlength{\parindent}{0pt}

\emph{Question~\ref{chk:hypotheses}.} Only $\{2,4,8,16,\dots\}$: $\{2,4\}$ is
finite (its sum $\tfrac25$ is rational), and for $\{2,4,6,\dots\}$ the sum
$\tfrac12+\tfrac14+\tfrac16+\dots$ is half of $1+\tfrac12+\tfrac13+\dots$,
which diverges.

\emph{Question~\ref{chk:positive}.} In the identity
$R(k)=((2^k-1)p-qJ(k))/q$ the numerator is an integer, so if it is
positive it is at least $1$, and $R(k)\ge1/q$. Choosing $k$ using $q$
leaves this argument intact, because $2^k-1$ and $J(k)$ are still
integers. The value zero would give no contradiction. Infinitude
supplies a selected $n>k$, whose multiplied term is positive and less
than $1$ and so contributes all of itself to $R(k)$, while the other
contributions are nonnegative.

\emph{Question~\ref{chk:columns}.} The exponents $A$ and the original
sum $S$ stay fixed, while the multiplier changes from $15$ to $255$, and
within a column every selected term is transformed by the same
multiplier. The row minima may come from different columns, and then no
single multiplier applied to $S$ produces them.

\emph{Question~\ref{chk:partial}.} A consecutive cycle has total below
$1$ and $n$ entries, hence mean below $1/n$. A sampled cycle selects the
smallest remainder from each group of equal size, and since the
fractional parts increase with the remainder, its mean is no larger,
even when it has fewer entries. The first value alone, however, is
$\tfrac{15}{63}=\tfrac5{21}=\tfrac{10}{42}$, which exceeds
$\tfrac16=\tfrac7{42}$. The bound for complete cycles may fail for a
beginning portion, and the separate bound~\eqref{eq:uniform-main} is
there to cover such portions.

\emph{Question~\ref{chk:order}.} No, since
$2Q'/T=128/512=1/4>1/10$. We must choose $T$ again: $T'=1024$ still
fails, but $T'=2048$ works, since $128/2048=1/16<1/10$. The middle
exponents $32,64,\dots,2048$ now have cycle lengths $1,1,2,4,8,16,32$,
so $M=64$ still works, although its validity had to be rechecked even
though its value did not change. This is why $Q$ is fixed before $T$.

\emph{Exercise~\ref{ex:2}.} The first five entries sum to $26/31$ and
the next two to $4/31$, so all seven average $30/217$. The two
piece-means, $26/155$ and $2/31$, must be weighted by their lengths,
$\bigl(5\cdot\tfrac{26}{155}+2\cdot\tfrac2{31}\bigr)/7=30/217$, since
equal weights for the two pieces would give each entry of the shorter
piece more weight than each entry of the longer one.

\emph{Exercise~\ref{ex:3}.} Both $3$ and $6$ divide $12$, so $7$ and
$63$ divide $2^{12}-1=4095$. The remainder of $12$ on division by $5$ is
$2$, so the remainder on division by $31$ is $2^2-1=3$; indeed
$4095=31\times132+3$.

\emph{Exercise~\ref{ex:4}.} For $k=3^j$, the exponents $3^i$ with
$i\le j$ divide $k$ and leave nothing. Each later exponent $n=3^i$
leaves less than $\tfrac2{2^{n-k}}$, and the gaps $3^i-3^j$ are
distinct and at least $2\times3^j$, so $R(3^j)<\sum_{e\ge2\times3^j}\tfrac2{2^e}=\tfrac4{2^{2\times3^j}}$,
which is below $\tfrac1q$ for large $j$.

\emph{Exercise~\ref{ex:5}.} We have $(\sqrt2-1)^2=3-2\sqrt2$,
$(\sqrt2-1)(3-2\sqrt2)=5\sqrt2-7$ and
$(a\sqrt2-c)(3-2\sqrt2)=(3a+2c)\sqrt2-(4a+3c)$. Starting from
$\sqrt2-1=1\sqrt2-1$, the last recurrence preserves nonnegative integer
values of $a$ and $c$, so every odd power $(\sqrt2-1)^{2i+1}$ is
$m\sqrt2-j$ for nonnegative integers $m$ and $j$, and it is positive.
Since $1^2<2<(3/2)^2$, we have $0<\sqrt2-1<\tfrac12$, and hence
$0<(\sqrt2-1)^{2i+1}<\tfrac1{2\cdot4^i}$, which is below $\tfrac1q$ once
$2\cdot4^i>q$. By the test, $\sqrt2$ is irrational.

\begin{samepage}
\emph{Exercise~\ref{ex:6}.} (a) With $P_0=1$, $a_j=P_{j-1}+1$ and
$P_j=P_{j-1}a_j$, so
$\tfrac1{P_{j-1}}-\tfrac1{P_j}=\tfrac{a_j-1}{P_j}=\tfrac1{a_j}$, and
adding these for $1,\dots,j$ leaves $1-\tfrac1{P_j}$. Since $P_j\ge2^j$,
these partial sums tend to $1$, so the infinite set $A$ satisfies the
reciprocal-sum hypothesis of the theorem. (b) The first $j$ exponents
divide $P_j$, and the exponent $P_j+1$ exceeds $k=P_j$, so it leaves
$\tfrac{2^{P_j}-1}{2^{P_j+1}-1}$, which is at least $\tfrac37$ because
$7(2^{P_j}-1)-3(2^{P_j+1}-1)=2^{P_j}-4\ge0$. Thus the particular
choices $k=P_j$ fail to make the remainders small. Other multiples of
$P_j$ preserve the same initial zeros, and averaging lets us search
among them.\par
\end{samepage}
